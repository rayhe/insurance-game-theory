Section I

The Facts

On December 4, 2024, UnitedHealthcare CEO Brian Thompson was shot and killed outside the Hilton Midtown in Manhattan. The words "delay," "deny," and "depose" were carved into the shell casings. Five days later, 26-year-old Luigi Mangione was arrested at a McDonald's in Altoona, Pennsylvania.

What followed was not mourning. An Emerson College poll found 41% of voters aged 18-29 considered the killing "acceptable." NORC found 70% of Americans blamed insurance denials and profits for creating the conditions that led to it. The shooting forced a question the industry had spent decades avoiding: whether its denial practices had engineered the conditions for their own destabilization.

Section II

The Game Theory

The Thompson shooting can be analyzed as a catastrophic failure in mechanism design. Leonid Hurwicz introduced the concept in the 1960s; the 2007 Nobel in Economics went to Hurwicz, Eric Maskin, and Roger Myerson "for having laid the foundations of mechanism design theory." The core question: given that people have private information and incentives to misrepresent it, what rules produce desirable outcomes? The health insurance appeals system is a mechanism. It was designed to resolve disputes. It failed. Every player acted rationally within their own framework, yet the system produced an outcome no party would have chosen ex ante.

Concept 1
Asymmetric Risk Tolerance

In standard negotiations, both parties have something to lose from escalation. This creates a natural boundary on conflict (Schelling, 1960). The health insurance model breaks this assumption for a specific population: people who have been denied life-saving or life-altering care. A terminal patient facing a denial has a fundamentally different utility function than a corporate actor. The marginal cost of extreme action approaches zero when expected remaining utility is already near zero. The industry priced this risk at approximately zero for decades.

Formally: let Ui(status quo) represent an individual's expected utility from continued cooperation. For a healthy policyholder, Ui >> 0 (future coverage is valuable), so the cost of defection Cd exceeds any benefit. For a terminally denied patient, Ui → 0, and the deterrence condition Cd > Bd may no longer hold. The industry's equilibrium required Ui >> 0 for all players. It did not.

Concept 2
Repeated Game to One-Shot Game

For a healthy person, health insurance functions as a repeated game. You cooperate now (pay premiums, tolerate bureaucracy) because you expect future benefit. This structure incentivizes compliance. For a person denied life-saving coverage, the game becomes one-shot. There are no future rounds. The cooperative incentive vanishes entirely. The insurer's entire strategy depended on treating all interactions as moves within a repeated game. Thompson's killing demonstrated that some interactions are terminal rounds, with no future to incentivize cooperation.

Concept 3
Credible Threat and Deterrence Failure

The industry's pre-shooting equilibrium implicitly assumed that the legal and social costs of violence were sufficient deterrents for all players. This assumption held for decades. But deterrence requires that the threatened party values what is at stake. A person facing denial of life-saving treatment, who has already exhausted appeals, is not deterrable in the conventional sense. The shooting established, empirically, that this population exists and that its members can act. The threat became credible through demonstration.

Concept 4
Mechanism Design Failure

Every complex system needs a release valve. In healthcare, the designated mechanisms were: internal appeals, external review, state insurance commissioners, CMS oversight, and the courts. These mechanisms existed. They also failed. When 82% of appeals succeed but only 0.2% of patients appeal, the mechanism is not functioning as designed. It is functioning as a filter that selects for the most persistent patients while allowing the vast majority of wrongful denials to stand unchallenged. In Hurwicz's terminology, the mechanism is not incentive compatible: it relies on patients bearing costs (time, expertise, emotional labor) that exceed the expected benefit of appealing, even when the appeal would succeed. The mechanism's designers knew this. The low appeal rate was not a failure to anticipate; it was a feature that kept costs down.

The failure is measurable on the provider side, too. A 2022 AMA survey of 1,000 physicians found that 94% said prior authorization demands at least sometimes lead to delays in care. Seven percent reported that the PA process "resulted in a serious adverse event leading to a patient's disability, permanent bodily damage, congenital anomaly, birth defect, or death." Eighty-eight percent said the number of drugs requiring PA had increased; 86% said the same for services. In oncology, the harm is particularly acute: ASCO's 2026 member survey found that 96% of oncologists report having patients who experienced harm from prior authorization, with 80% reporting outright disease progression directly attributable to PA delays. The mechanisms that were supposed to prevent harm were themselves causing it.

In June 2026, the HHS Office of Inspector General quantified the pattern across 19 Medicare Advantage organizations covering 29.3 million enrollees. CVS Health denied 80% of long-term acute care hospital admission requests. Humana denied 72%. UnitedHealth denied 71%. For inpatient rehabilitation, UnitedHealth led at 66%. The 16 remaining insurers averaged 42% and 41%, respectively. When enrollees appealed skilled nursing facility denials, 95% were overturned. The denials were almost universally wrong.

The trail led directly to naviHealth, UnitedHealth's Optum subsidiary. naviHealth processed half of all skilled nursing admission requests and denied 14%, compared to 11% for internal review and 9% for other contractors. Ninety-seven percent of naviHealth's SNF denials were overturned on appeal. For long-term care hospitals and rehab facilities, naviHealth's denial rates were 73% and 68%. "Differences in denial rates between for-profit and nonprofit [Medicare Advantage insurers] suggest that financial incentives may be partially driving higher denial rates," OIG wrote. The Inspector General was saying, with careful bureaucratic phrasing, what the class action alleged: the algorithm optimizes for money, not medicine.

The shooting was what happens when the designated release valves are inadequate: pressure finds another outlet.

Concept 5
Information Asymmetry Collapse

Kenneth Arrow's foundational 1963 paper demonstrated that medical care markets deviate from competitive ideals precisely because of information asymmetry between providers, insurers, and patients. George Akerlof's "Market for Lemons" (1970) showed that information asymmetry can destroy entire markets when buyers cannot distinguish quality. The prior authorization system created an analogous dynamic: patients cannot distinguish a clinically justified denial from a financially motivated one, so they treat all denials as potentially illegitimate. The system weaponized this asymmetry. Before the shooting, insurers maintained informational advantages that made the denial strategy viable: proprietary algorithms (nH Predict at UnitedHealth, PxDx at Cigna), opaque denial criteria, deliberately complex appeals processes. Patients could not observe the decision criteria, could not evaluate whether a denial was clinically justified, and faced prohibitive costs in challenging it. This is the Rothschild-Stiglitz (1976) adverse selection problem inverted: instead of insurers suffering from information disadvantage vis-à-vis policyholders, the insurers used their informational advantage against the very people they were supposed to protect.

The shooting triggered forced disclosure. A Senate report on denial practices, the class action revealing the alleged 90% error rate, public data requirements under the 2024 Interoperability and Prior Authorization Rule (first reports published March 2026), and KFF's analysis of insurer-reported denial rates all followed. The information asymmetry that enabled the old equilibrium has been structurally reduced, though not eliminated.

Notably, algorithmic denial was not unique to UnitedHealth. In 2023, a separate class action alleged that Cigna's PxDx system allowed medical directors to deny claims in batches, spending an average of 1.2 seconds per review. The pattern was industry-wide; Thompson's killing merely made UnitedHealth the focal point.

Concept 6
The Prisoner's Dilemma of Reform

Individual insurers face a classic collective action problem (Olson, 1965). The insurer that reforms most aggressively loses margin to competitors who reform less. The June 2025 voluntary pledge attempts to solve this by coordinating simultaneous reform across nearly 60 insurers. But the pledge is voluntary and AHIP refused to release the underlying survey data. Without verification, each insurer has an incentive to free-ride: announce reform while maintaining aggressive denial practices. The 11% reduction in prior auth requirements is self-reported and unaudited. Whether it represents genuine reform or a strategic minimum to defuse public pressure is unknowable with current data.

Section III

The New Equilibrium

The pre-shooting equilibrium (aggressive denial with no personal risk to decision-makers) is no longer stable. In Nash's formulation (1950), a stable state requires that no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing strategy. The shooting introduced a new strategy into the game that was previously absent from the payoff matrix. A new Nash equilibrium must account for a demonstrated non-zero probability of violence against executives. The question is what form this equilibrium takes.

Three Possible Stable States

Industry Response Pathways and Their Costs

Path A: Genuine Denial Reform

Reduce denial rates to clinically defensible levels. Eliminate or restructure AI-driven denial systems. Make appeals automatic rather than patient-initiated. This path directly addresses the asymmetric risk by reducing the population of desperate, denied patients. Cost: reduced margins. The shareholder lawsuit reveals that markets treat "being nicer to patients" as a profitability risk. Reform means structurally lower earnings.

Path B: Security Fortress

Maintain denial practices but invest heavily in executive protection. UNH spent $1.7M in 2024; industry-wide security spending has doubled. The problem: this scales linearly with threat while doing nothing about root cause. At some point the security cost exceeds the savings from aggressive denial. Additionally, it only protects individuals, not the industry's political viability. The DHS report confirms the threat set is expanding beyond healthcare executives to government officials.

Path C: Regulatory Capitulation

Government mandates reform. The 2024 Interoperability and Prior Authorization Rule requires public disclosure of denial metrics starting in 2026. CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz explicitly linked reform urgency to the shooting. If voluntary reform proves insufficient, mandatory denial rate caps or automatic approval requirements become politically viable. The industry loses control of the pace and scope of change.

The Guilty Plea Paradox

On August 14, 2026, Mangione pled guilty to two federal stalking charges with no deal. "I shot Mr. Thompson in Manhattan," he told U.S. District Judge Margaret Garnett. "I understood that my actions would place him in fear of death or bodily injury. I knew what I was doing was illegal." Prosecutors are seeking life in prison; sentencing guidelines indicate a range of 24 to 30 years. Sentencing is set for December 18, 2026.

"Today's guilty plea marks an important step toward justice for Brian and for our family. While nothing will ease the pain of losing him, we are grateful that the federal justice system has held the person responsible for this heinous act accountable." Thompson family statement, August 14, 2026 (People / USA Today)

His attorneys immediately filed to dismiss the state murder charges on double jeopardy grounds. Their core claim: that state and federal prosecutors coordinated from the start. "From the day of his arrest on December 9, 2024, Luigi Mangione has been a pawn in parallel prosecutions pursued by the New York County District Attorney's Office and the United States Department of Justice," the defense wrote. New York's double jeopardy statute goes further than the U.S. Constitution, barring two prosecutions "based upon the same act or criminal transaction," even between separate sovereigns. There is precedent. In 2019, a New York judge dismissed state charges against former Trump campaign chairman Paul Manafort after his defense lawyer argued the state residential mortgage fraud prosecution covered the same conduct as his federal bank fraud conviction. But precedent also runs against Mangione: Derek Chauvin was prosecuted in both state and federal courts for the killing of George Floyd, and the Marine Midland Bank robbers' dual prosecution survived a double jeopardy challenge before New York's highest court.

Three days later, on August 17, Judge Carro postponed the September 8 trial indefinitely, giving prosecutors until October 9 to respond to the double jeopardy motion. The defense then has until October 30 for its reply. Carro set the next court appearance for December 10. Legal experts are split. Anthony Capozzolo, a former federal and state prosecutor, says it is "more likely than not" the state case goes away because "both offenses will be punishing someone for the murder of the same victim." Bennett Gershman, a law professor at Pace University, disagrees: "The elements in stalking and the elements of murder are very, very distinguishable." New York's double jeopardy law carves out exceptions for crimes that have different elements or are aimed at preventing "very different kinds of harm or evil." The federal stalking charges require intent to kill but do not require that the death occurs. Whether that distinction is sufficient to survive CPL §40.20 is now the central legal question. DA Bragg's office says it is "prepared to litigate." Five Pennsylvania charges remain pending.

In his courtroom statement, Mangione described suffering a broken back and watching others endure similar denials of care. He explained how he posed as an investor at a firm managing more than $50 million to learn about UNH's investor conference, receiving an immediate response. But his most revealing observation was about the conference itself: he had researched an event hosted by "America's largest health care organization with the stated mission to make the health system work better for everyone," and found it would be attended by "company executives, the board of directors, and hundreds of investors, not doctors, nurses, and patients." He made part of the gun using a 3D printer. Thompson's wife, Paulette, wiped away tears in the front row as Mangione spoke.

The allocution contained, almost incidentally, a precise map of the principal-agent problem. An organization whose stated mission is improving healthcare convened its most important annual gathering for capital, not for clinicians or the people its mission is supposed to serve. And when Mangione contacted UHC as a patient navigating "the obstacles of the health insurance system," the system responded at the pace patients know. When he emailed as an investor, he "received an immediate response within an hour." The response-time differential is a measurable signal of institutional priority. The system is optimized to respond to capital with the urgency it denies to patients. Mangione didn't need game theory to see this. He just emailed them twice.

In game-theoretic terms, the guilty plea with no deal is a signal of commitment. It demonstrates that the actor accepted the maximum personal cost, which strengthens rather than weakens the credible threat for others in similar positions. A rational actor seeking leniency would have bargained. An actor demonstrating that the cause was worth any personal cost sends a different signal entirely. NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch responded directly: "Luigi Mangione carried out a ruthless act of violence that shocked this city. A killer is not a hero." The statement was itself a game-theoretic move, an attempt to reshape the narrative framing. That it needed to be said at all is the point.

In Schelling's commitment theory, Mangione burned his ships. No deal means no implicit message of regret. The message is: "I did this, I knew the cost, and I accepted it."

Game Theory Concept
Response-Time Asymmetry as Principal-Agent Signal

In a well-functioning agency relationship, the agent responds to the principal's needs. UnitedHealthcare's stated principal is the patient; its actual principal, revealed by resource allocation, is the investor. Mangione's allocution documented both sides: as a patient navigating "the obstacles of the health insurance system," the response was slow; as a fake investor managing $50M+, it was instantaneous. Jensen and Meckling's (1976) theory of the firm predicts exactly this divergence: when the agent's compensation is tied to one class of principals (shareholders) but its stated mission invokes another (patients), resource allocation follows compensation, not mission. The investor conference roster, "company executives, the board of directors, and hundreds of investors, not doctors, nurses, and patients," is what Jensen and Meckling would call the residual claimant revelation: the annual gathering shows who the organization actually serves. The response-time differential shows how urgently it serves them.

Concept 7
The Moral Hazard of Martyrdom

The public response to the shooting created a moral hazard the industry did not anticipate. Mark Pauly's 1968 paper established the concept of moral hazard in health insurance: when people are shielded from costs, they consume more. The post-Thompson variant inverts this. Here, the moral hazard runs toward the industry: when the social cost of retaliatory violence is reduced by public sympathy, the probability of future acts increases. Forty-one percent of young Americans found the killing acceptable. Mangione became a folk hero. T-shirts, fan clubs, and memes proliferated. The social cost of the act, which deterrence theory assumes is high, turned out to be low or even negative for a significant portion of the population. This inverts the standard deterrence calculus. If society partially rewards the act, the cost function changes for potential future actors.

The Deductible Death Spiral: Cost-Sharing as Population-Level Harm

The prior authorization denial is the visible mechanism. Behind it sits a quieter one that compounds the harm: the cost-sharing structure itself. Three peer-reviewed studies published between 2025 and 2026 quantify what had long been intuited. Insurance design features that shift costs to patients do not merely increase financial hardship. They increase mortality.

Barnes et al. (2026) analyzed data from over 147,000 respondents to the National Health Interview Survey and found that cancer survivors enrolled in high-deductible health plans had 46% worse overall survival (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.19-1.79) and 34% worse cancer-specific survival (HR 1.34, 95% CI 1.01-1.77) compared to those in standard plans. The finding held across income levels. Even cancer survivors with household incomes above 400% of the federal poverty level, who could in theory absorb higher out-of-pocket costs, showed 65% worse cancer-specific survival (HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.16-2.36). Financial barriers, including delayed care, foregone treatment, and medication underuse, mediated the association. Among adults without cancer, high-deductible plans showed no significant survival effect. The mortality gradient is specific to patients who need sustained care: precisely the population that encounters prior authorization most frequently.

A parallel finding emerged from Roberts et al. (2025) in the New England Journal of Medicine. Among nearly one million low-income Medicare beneficiaries who lost Medicaid coverage, and with it the Medicare Part D Low-Income Subsidy worth approximately $6,200 annually, mortality increased 4% compared to those who retained coverage longer. For patients on HIV antiretroviral therapy, the increase was 22%. Over 2,900 people died during the study period. More than half of those who lost Medicaid regained eligibility within a year, revealing that the coverage losses were driven not by genuine changes in eligibility but by administrative complexity: paperwork failures, redetermination backlogs, processing errors. The system was not removing ineligible people. It was losing eligible ones.

The third study, published in JAMA Psychiatry in March 2026, found that health insurance disenrollment among patients being treated for opioid use disorder was associated with a 51% increase in all-cause mortality hazards and a 56% increase in overdose mortality. All-cause mortality was 4.34 times higher for disenrolled patients compared to those who remained enrolled and in treatment. The study followed over 20,000 patients across three integrated health systems. The finding connects directly to the Parity Paradox documented earlier: behavioral health patients, already subject to the highest PA denial rates and the weakest federal enforcement, face a second mortality filter when they lose coverage entirely.

Game Theory Concept
Arrow's Risk Transfer, Inverted

Arrow's foundational 1963 paper demonstrated that the socially optimal health insurance contract transfers risk from the risk-averse patient to the risk-neutral insurer. High-deductible health plans invert this. They shift risk back to the patient through deductibles and cost-sharing, creating a mechanism where the patient bears precisely the uncertainty that insurance was designed to absorb. Arrow proved this transfer was inefficient. Barnes et al. proved it is lethal. The HDHP is not insurance in the Arrovian sense. It is a financial instrument that maintains the appearance of risk pooling while restoring the pre-insurance distribution of risk, with one difference: the patient pays a premium for the privilege of bearing it.

The connection to prior authorization runs deeper than parallel harm. Consider the patient journey through the denial system. A patient whose claim survives the PA process, navigating the denial, the appeal, the peer-to-peer review, still faces the deductible. The Barnes data show that this second financial barrier produces its own independent mortality effect, one that operates after the PA system has already filtered out the patients who abandoned their claims. The two mechanisms function as sequential filters: PA denial removes a proportion of patients from care (the 0.2% appeal rate demonstrates the attrition), and cost-sharing removes a further proportion of those who survive the first filter. The combined effect is multiplicative, not additive.

The Medicaid churn data from Roberts et al. reveals a structural analog to the PA denial mechanism itself. Both operate through administrative complexity rather than clinical judgment. A prior authorization denial requires the patient to navigate a bureaucratic process: fax forms, wait for review, schedule a peer-to-peer call. The system's designers know that a large fraction will not complete it. Medicaid redetermination operates identically: the beneficiary must navigate paperwork, submit documentation, and meet deadlines, and the system knows that over half of those who lose coverage were still eligible. In both cases, the mechanism's value to the system is not the clinical appropriateness of the outcome but the attrition rate of the process. Administrative friction is not a bug in the implementation. It is the product being sold.

The policy implication is that PA reform alone addresses only one stage of a multi-stage attrition system. Gold card programs, electronic PA mandates, and the CMS-0057-F transparency rules all target the insurer-provider interaction at the point of authorization. They do not reach the patient-insurer interaction at the point of payment. As long as a patient who wins the PA battle still faces a $7,500 deductible (the average for an ACA bronze plan in 2026), the cost-sharing mechanism will continue to produce its own independent mortality gradient. The system does not need to deny the claim. It only needs to approve it and let the bill do the rest.

The Defense Pivot: Strategy Switching as Sequential Signaling

Before the guilty plea, the defense team executed a remarkable strategic reversal that is itself a lesson in game theory. On June 17, Judge Carro disclosed that Mangione's lawyers had filed notice of an affirmative psychiatric defense: they intended to argue that Mangione was suffering from "extreme emotional disturbance" at the time of the shooting. If a jury accepted the claim, a murder conviction would be reduced to manslaughter, with a maximum of 25 years instead of life. The defense had informed Carro of this intent months earlier, in September 2025, and the judge had held sealed hearings to evaluate it.

Twenty-four hours later, on June 18, the defense withdrew the EED notice in a one-line letter. Former Manhattan prosecutor Gary Galperin called it a "stunning reversal." The timing was not coincidental. Carro had ordered the defense to share the specifics of Mangione's psychiatric condition with prosecutors immediately: "They need to know what the malady is that this defendant suffers and how that triggered extreme emotional distress," Carro said. "I'm not going to let you surprise the People on the eve of trial. Get it done." Prosecutors had complained of "stonewalling" and receiving "nothing" about Mangione's psychiatric state.

The game-theoretic reading is straightforward. The EED defense was a credible threat so long as its details remained private. Its value to the defense was informational asymmetry: prosecutors could not prepare a rebuttal without knowing the specific psychiatric claim, and the sealed proceedings kept the defense's cards hidden. The moment Carro forced disclosure, the expected payoff changed. Either the psychiatric evidence was insufficiently strong to survive prosecution scrutiny (in which case revealing it would weaken the defense and produce a failed trial strategy), or revealing it in state court would compromise federal plea negotiations that were already underway. CNN reported in late June that Mangione's attorneys had discussed plea possibilities with federal prosecutors, though no agreement was reached at that time. The EED withdrawal eliminated the risk of creating state-court psychiatric admissions that could complicate a federal resolution.

The sequence from June through August maps onto a three-stage signaling game. In stage one, the defense signals type θ₁ (psychiatrically mitigated actor) by filing the EED notice. This shapes the prosecution's trial preparation, the court's procedural posture, and the media narrative. In stage two, when forced to convert private information into public evidence, the defense withdraws and signals type θ₂ (strategic rational actor exploring resolution). In stage three, the federal guilty plea on August 14 completes the pivot: instead of arguing diminished responsibility in state court, the defense admits full responsibility in federal court and immediately weaponizes that admission against the state case via double jeopardy.

The pivot's sophistication lies in what it revealed about the defense's private valuation of the two forums. State court offered a potential manslaughter verdict (maximum 25 years) via EED, but required disclosing psychiatric evidence that might fail under scrutiny and could prejudice both the state and federal juries. Federal court, after the murder charges were dismissed in January 2026, offered the stalking charges as a vehicle for a plea that would trigger New York's unusually broad double jeopardy protections. DA Seidemann anticipated this. In a letter to state Judge Carro before the plea hearing, he warned that "if a proposed federal guilty plea would operate to defeat a just outcome in the state prosecution, the People could advise the federal district court of that consequence, and the court should consider that fact in deciding whether to accept the plea." The letter was itself a strategic signal: the DA attempting to influence Judge Garnett's willingness to accept a plea by threatening to raise objections in federal court. She accepted it anyway.

Game Theory Concept
Type Switching in Games of Incomplete Information (Harsanyi, 1967-68)

In Harsanyi's formalization of Bayesian games, each player has a "type" that encodes their private information. Other players assign probability distributions over possible types and update beliefs based on observed actions. The EED withdrawal is a case of deliberate type switching: the defense initially signaled type θ₁ (psychiatrically mitigated), inducing the prosecution to prepare for a diminished-capacity trial. When forced to reveal the private information underlying that type, the defense pivoted to type θ₂ (strategically rational, seeking resolution via plea). Each signal was rational given the information environment at the time. The 24-hour reversal did not reflect indecision. It reflected a precise recalculation of expected payoffs once the information asymmetry that sustained the EED strategy was eliminated by judicial order. Harsanyi received the 1994 Nobel Prize alongside Nash and Selten for this framework, which underpins modern analysis of bargaining, negotiation, and strategic litigation.

The Visibility Game: Camera Access as Jury Nullification Infrastructure

Before the guilty plea rendered it temporarily moot, the defense fought a parallel battle over the information environment of the state trial itself. On July 31, 2026, Mangione's lead attorney Karen Friedman Agnifilo sent Judge Carro a letter challenging the court's media access plan for the September trial. The plan limited coverage to 68 journalists, restricted jury selection to six reporters, and offered no overflow courtroom for the duration of proceedings. Agnifilo demanded the trial be broadcast live. "Criminal defendants have a Sixth Amendment right to a fully public, open and unbiased trial," the defense wrote. "This means that every member of the public and the press who wishes to see the Mangione trial should be permitted to do so." The defense noted it was not consulted on the media plan and only learned of its details from an independent journalist who posted the document on X.

The demand inverts the standard defendant-publicity dynamic. In conventional criminal proceedings, defense attorneys seek to minimize media exposure. Pretrial publicity biases jury pools toward conviction. Cameras pressure jurors to deliver the verdict the public expects. Defendants with good legal defenses have every incentive to restrict the audience to twelve people in a box. Mangione's defense wants the opposite: maximum visibility, maximum audience, maximum social pressure on the factfinder. Former federal prosecutor Neama Rahmani identified the strategy explicitly. "Mangione is probably the most popular accused murderer that I've ever covered in the past 25 years," he told Fox News. "Has a fan base. There's upwards of 10 to 20 percent of the people who have been surveyed who believe that even if he did it, it was justified. And I think the defense knows this, and they know that more attention creates the possibility of jury nullification, or maybe even an acquittal." The defense, he said, is "counting on influencers to do what a courtroom sketch never could: keep the case viral."

The prosecution responded with a signal of its own. On August 10, prosecutors sent Judge Carro a letter voicing concern about Mangione's supporters and warning that an overflow room could invite "spectator misconduct," with fans attempting to intimidate witnesses or jurors. This was the same office that had already secured an anonymous jury order (requested because of "fan intimidation" concerns), a measure typically reserved for organized crime trials. Carro's compromise: an overflow room with a live feed inside the courthouse, but no external broadcast. Cameras remain banned under New York Civil Rights Law section 52, and the defense's constitutional challenge to the statute did not succeed.

The camera fight reveals each side's private assessment of the jury pool. When a defendant seeks more publicity, they are signaling that the marginal juror is more sympathetic than the median voter, that popular sentiment favors acquittal or leniency so strongly that the social cost of conviction exceeds the social cost of nullification. When prosecutors seek less publicity, they are confirming that assessment. The anonymous jury order is the clearest tell: the state acknowledged, through a formal judicial mechanism, that Mangione's supporters represent a credible threat to the integrity of the verdict. The defense's response was to demand that the very visibility prosecutors feared be maximized, converting the court's own protective measure into evidence for expanding public access.

The camera debate connects directly to the coordination dynamics documented in the defense fund analysis. The $1.6 million raised from 40,000 donors at a median of $15 solved Olson's collective action problem outside the courtroom. The live broadcast request was an attempt to extend that coordination inside it: visible public sympathy would lower the social cost of nullification for any individual juror, while the absence of cameras would restore the private-game structure where jurors face only the twelve-person social dynamics of deliberation. Jury nullification is itself a coordination problem. A single holdout juror faces enormous social pressure from eleven peers. When that juror can look beyond the deliberation room and see tens of millions of Americans who consider the defendant a folk hero, the calculus shifts. The defense was not seeking transparency for its own sake. It was seeking to modify the payoff structure of the jury game by changing who was watching.

Game Theory Concept
Audience Costs and Information Environment Design (Fearon, 1994)

Fearon's domestic audience costs model, originally developed for international crises, demonstrates that publicly observable actions change equilibrium behavior by introducing reputational payoffs that do not exist in private settings. When a leader makes a public threat, backing down becomes costly because the domestic audience punishes perceived weakness. Applied to the Mangione trial: the defense's demand for cameras is a demand to shift the game from private (twelve jurors, sealed deliberation, anonymous verdict) to public (millions of observers, viral commentary, social accountability). In a private game, a juror's payoff depends only on their assessment of the evidence and the social dynamics of the deliberation room. In a public game, a juror who convicts a popular defendant faces audience costs: social disapproval, online backlash, and the knowledge that their decision is visible to a sympathetic public. The prosecution's request to minimize publicity is the mirror move: keeping the game private preserves the equilibrium where conviction depends on evidence rather than audience sentiment. Carro's compromise, an overflow room without broadcast, represents a partial information structure: the trial is more observable than a closed courtroom but less observable than a livestream. The equilibrium shifts proportionally. This explains why jury nullification, a phenomenon that legal scholars treat as rare and unpredictable, becomes strategically pursuable once the defense can control the information environment. The camera is not a passive recording device. It is a mechanism that transforms the game's payoff matrix.

The December Calendar: Backward Induction in Real Time

The scheduling order that followed the guilty plea created a sequential game of unusual transparency. On August 17, Judge Carro postponed the September 8 state murder trial and set three dates: October 9, when Manhattan prosecutors must respond to the double jeopardy motion; October 30, when the defense must file its reply; and December 10, when the court will reconvene to hear arguments and potentially rule. Eight days later, on December 18, Judge Garnett will sentence Mangione in federal court. The December calendar is a backward induction problem with the solution visible to all players before the game begins.

Work backward from the end. On December 18, Garnett decides between the 24-to-30-year sentencing guidelines and the life sentence prosecutors are seeking. Her calculus depends, in part, on whether a state murder trial is still pending. If Carro dismisses the state charges on December 10, Garnett knows she is imposing the only punishment Mangione will face for the killing. If Carro denies the double jeopardy motion, a separate trial with its own potential life sentence remains on the horizon. The two outcomes create different payoff structures for the same sentencing decision. A rational judge internalizes the existence or absence of a parallel proceeding.

DA Bragg faces the inverse problem. His office has said it is "prepared to litigate the defense motions." But litigation is costly and uncertain. If Garnett imposes a life sentence on December 18, the marginal value of a state murder conviction drops toward zero: Mangione would already face the maximum penalty. Attorney Mark Zauderer told the Wall Street Journal that the DA's position may be hard to maintain because "it is clear that both the federal and state statutes, although different, were designed to punish the taking of human life." If the DA is satisfied with the federal sentence, dismissing the state charges becomes the rational move regardless of the double jeopardy ruling. The state case becomes a sunk cost that yields no additional deterrence.

A deeper paradox sits inside the legal record. When Garnett threw out the federal murder and weapons charges in January 2026, she ruled that the underlying stalking offense did not qualify as a "crime of violence." Retired NYPD Inspector Paul Mauro identified the implication: "The federal judge has already found, thanks to Mangione's own arguments, that stalking is not a 'crime of violence.' And if that's the federal position, then you have to ask yourself, is the harm of a nonviolent crime... different enough from what the statute for murder 2 is trying to prevent so that you can say the two statutes are distinct enough that the state case could go forward." Mangione's defense won a ruling that weakened his own double jeopardy claim. In game theory, this is a move with negative externalities across time periods: what was optimal in Round 1 (remove the death penalty) creates risk in Round 2 (preserve the DA's argument that the statutes protect different interests).

The allocution itself is an irrevocable commitment with dual-use properties. Seth Zuckerman, a New York criminal defense attorney and former prosecutor, told People that with the guilty plea, "it's a stronger argument to say he's already been convicted in the federal system, and therefore, there's a change in circumstance here, and the state case can't proceed." But Zuckerman identified the embedded risk: if the double jeopardy motion fails, "they may try to use his sworn plea allocution against him in the case." Mangione stood under oath and said, "I shot Mr. Thompson in Manhattan." If the state trial proceeds, prosecutors no longer need to prove he pulled the trigger. He told them. The allocution is a Schelling commitment device that works in both directions: it strengthens the double jeopardy argument precisely because it is irrevocable, but its irrevocability means it becomes evidence for the other side if the strategy fails. The rational actor must have assessed the probability of double jeopardy dismissal as high enough to justify the risk of self-incrimination.

The December 10 / December 18 calendar makes one additional prediction. If Carro rules before Garnett sentences, and his ruling is immediately appealed (Zuckerman: "I presume whoever loses the double jeopardy motion is going to go to the Appellate Division for an emergency appeal"), the state case enters limbo. An appeal would stay the state trial regardless of Carro's ruling. Garnett would then sentence Mangione in federal court while the state case's existence remains uncertain. This is the game's third equilibrium: not resolution, but sustained indeterminacy, where the federal sentence becomes the de facto final outcome while the state case formally survives in a suspended state. Rational actors on all sides may prefer this ambiguity. Bragg avoids a humiliating dismissal. The defense avoids a state trial. And the system avoids a precedent that either strengthens or weakens New York's unusually broad double jeopardy statute.

Game Theory Concept
Subgame Perfect Equilibrium in Sequential Legal Proceedings

In a finite sequential game, Zermelo's theorem (1913) and Selten's refinement (1965) establish that rational players can solve the game by backward induction: start from the last move, determine the optimal choice, then work backward through each preceding decision node. The December 2026 calendar is a real-world instance. Garnett's sentencing decision (Dec 18) depends on Carro's double jeopardy ruling (Dec 10), which depends on the defense reply (Oct 30), which depends on the DA's response (Oct 9), which depends on the strength of the double jeopardy claim, which depends on the nature of the guilty plea (Aug 14). Each player can observe the previous moves, the decision tree is finite, and the payoffs (sentence length, prosecution resources, precedent value) are partially known. The game's unusual feature is that all players are simultaneously institutional actors with reputational concerns and agents with statutory mandates. A judge cannot choose the outcome that produces the most "efficient" result if the law directs otherwise. This constrains the backward induction solution: legal mandates introduce binding constraints that narrow the strategy space below what pure payoff maximization would predict. The result is a subgame perfect equilibrium that may differ from the socially optimal outcome, because legal institutions were not designed to optimize the Mangione-specific game.

The State Legislative Wave

While the voluntary pledge was aspirational, state legislatures responded with binding law. By mid-2026, more than 25 states had introduced over 35 bills specifically regulating insurers' use of AI in coverage decisions. The first wave came in 2025, when six states enacted legislation: Arizona (HB 2175), Illinois (SB 1425), Indiana (HB 1271), Maryland (HB 820), Nebraska (LB 77), and Texas (SB 815). Indiana's law went further than prior auth, prohibiting insurers from using AI as the sole basis to downcode any claim without physician review of the patient's medical record.

The 2026 wave was larger and faster. Seven additional state laws were enacted by July 2026, bringing the total to fourteen enacted laws across thirteen states. Alabama (SB 63, effective October 1, 2026) bars AI-only coverage decisions and mandates disclosure when AI is used in the review process. Colorado (HB 1139, signed by Governor Polis on June 2, 2026) covers insurers, pharmacy benefit managers, private utilization review organizations, and managed care entities, requiring AI to base decisions on individual clinical history rather than group data and mandating periodic audits for accuracy. Georgia (SB 444, effective January 2027) prohibits insurance coverage decisions based solely on AI systems. Iowa (HF 2635, effective July 1, 2026) allows AI for initial review but prohibits it as the sole basis for denial, delay, or downgrade. Washington (SB 5395, effective June 11, 2026) explicitly prohibits AI as the sole means to deny, delay, or modify care. Illinois passed a second law, SB 3114 (effective January 2028), targeting claim downcoding: it bans any algorithm that bypasses billing professional submissions to downcode a claim and prohibits targeting clinicians who treat complex or chronic patients. Minnesota passed a bipartisan ban on AI-only prior authorization decisions through both chambers by wide margins in May 2026; the bill awaits Governor Walz's signature, with an effective date of January 2027.

The political alignments are striking. Florida Governor Ron DeSantis, a Republican, rolled out an "AI Bill of Rights" that includes restrictions on AI in insurance claims processing and authorizes state regulators to inspect insurer algorithms. In Democratic-led Maryland and Illinois, lawmakers pursued similar bans. The bipartisan nature of this response reflects the depth of public anger: restricting algorithmic denials is one of the few issues where red and blue states legislate in the same direction.

A federal counter-current complicates matters. President Trump's December 2025 executive order on AI seeks to preempt most state AI regulation, warning that "excessive State regulation thwarts" American competitiveness. The order proposes restricting federal funding or pursuing litigation against states that enact overly burdensome AI rules. Whether federal preemption will override state insurance regulation remains legally untested, but the tension creates a new dimension in the game: states legislating faster than the federal government can preempt them.

The policy incoherence runs deeper than federalism. While Congress advances five bills to restrict prior authorization in Medicare Advantage, the CMS Innovation Center did the opposite: it launched the "Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction" (WISeR) Model, which expands PA requirements in traditional Medicare, outsourcing approvals to six private technology companies across six states. The House introduced H.Res.704, a bipartisan resolution formally opposing the model. The resolution's text cited the KFF finding that 81.7% of Medicare Advantage denials are overturned on appeal and referenced the 90% AI error rate alleged in the class action suits. But CMS launched WISeR anyway on January 15, 2026, in Arizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas, and Washington. One arm of the federal government is expanding the same system that another arm is dismantling. In game-theoretic terms, this is not a past coordination failure but an ongoing one.

California may offer the most aggressive state-level model. SB 363, introduced by Sen. Scott Wiener, imposes escalating fines on insurers whose denial overturn rates exceed 50%: at least $50,000 for the first violation, up to $400,000 for the second, and a minimum of $1 million for each subsequent case. In 2023, 72% of California appeals filed with the Department of Managed Health Care resulted in denial reversals. The bill also mandates the first-ever public reporting of denial rates by care category (medical, behavioral, surgical) and by age, with insurer-specific data published in annual reports. Reporting requirements took effect June 1, 2026. If the penalty provisions survive a Newsom veto, California would join Connecticut as one of the only states forcing insurers to face financial consequences for systematic wrongful denials.

Federal Legislative Momentum

Congress is moving in the same direction as the states, but through different channels. In July 2026, the Improving Seniors' Timely Access to Care Act of 2026 passed two House committees unanimously in a single week: Ways and Means by a 42-0 recorded vote on July 15, and Energy and Commerce by a 45-0 vote on July 21. The bill now advances to the House floor. It has 298 House cosponsors and 71 Senate cosponsors, making it the most widely supported bipartisan healthcare legislation in the 119th Congress. The Congressional Budget Office determined it would have zero impact on federal spending. More than 300 national and state organizations, including physician groups, patient advocates, and even some insurers, have endorsed it. The AMA called the vote "an idea with bipartisan support and political legs" and urged House leadership to bring it to the floor before the August recess. ASCO reported that 96% of surveyed oncologists had patients harmed by prior authorization, with 80% reporting disease progression directly attributable to PA delays. The Regulatory Relief Coalition, representing 16 national physician organizations, called it evidence that providers and patients have "seen little meaningful change" from voluntary pledges alone.

The legislative pipeline runs five bills deep, each attacking the problem from a different angle. The Stop Deadly Denials Act (H.R. 8377, Khanna/Jayapal, April 2026) would ban prior authorization entirely in Medicare Advantage and prohibit AI-only coverage determinations. The bipartisan Prior Authorization Accountability Act (Goldman, passed Energy and Commerce Committee July 2026) requires all insurers to publicly post denial rates, appeal outcomes, and processing times on their websites and plan materials. The Patient Refunds for Bad Denials Act (Craig/Ryan) creates financial penalties of $10 million plus $2 million per percentage point above a 25% denial threshold, with penalties paid directly to affected enrollees. And the Medicare Advantage Improvement Act of 2026 (Joyce, introduced April 20) may be the most mechanism-design-savvy of the lot: it establishes a compliance scoring system from 0 to 100 for MA plans, with automatic 2% payment cuts for low-scoring plans that feed into star ratings. It mandates 72-hour standard PA response times and 24-hour expedited decisions, and it bans third-party vendors that routinely automate denials in medical necessity reviews. Five of the bill's cosponsors are themselves physicians. Together, these five bills represent a full spectrum of reform ambition: transparency (Accountability Act), standardization (Timely Access Act), punishment (Patient Refunds Act), enforcement (MA Improvement Act), and abolition (Stop Deadly Denials).

Separately, HHS estimates that the CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Rule (CMS-0057-F) will save physician practices approximately $15 billion over ten years through electronic streamlining alone. Beginning in 2026, insurers must share specific explanations for PA denials. Starting in 2027, plans must support electronic PA embedded within physicians' EHR systems. These are not voluntary pledges. They are rules with compliance deadlines.

The Transparency Trap: What the Data Actually Shows

The CMS-0057-F transparency rule was designed to end informational asymmetry. But the first wave of compliance data reveals a subtler game: insurers can comply with disclosure requirements while strategically aggregating numbers to obscure what actually happens at the contract level. An independent extraction of CMS-0057-F per-contract prior authorization data, analyzing 93 Medicare Advantage contracts across UnitedHealthcare (61 contracts) and Humana (32 contracts) covering 18.4 million prior authorization requests, found that UnitedHealthcare's volume-weighted denial rate was 13.5%. That contradicts UNH's self-reported headline of "95.4% approved."

The discrepancy is not fraud. It is strategic aggregation. UNH's headline number includes routine requests (lab draws, standard office visits) that are approved automatically and require no clinical review. When those bulk approvals are included in the denominator, the denial rate for clinically consequential services disappears into a reassuring aggregate. The per-contract data tells a different story: denial rates ranged from 0.7% to 25.2% across UNH's 61 contracts, a 36-fold spread. Some contracts approve nearly everything. Others deny one in four requests. The patients assigned to the high-denial contracts experience a fundamentally different insurance product than those in the low-denial ones, even though all carry the same UnitedHealthcare brand.

Humana's data showed similar patterns, with appeal overturn rates of 64.7% compared to UNH's 57.9%. Both figures confirm that a majority of appealed denials were wrong. But the real finding is the denominator: approximately 3 million Medicare Advantage patients per year are denied care they are entitled to receive and never appeal. They absorb the denial as final. This is the mechanism design failure in quantified form: the transparency rule generated data that proves the system denies entitled care at scale, but the data format allows insurers to present the numbers in ways that suggest otherwise.

A peer-reviewed Health Affairs study independently confirmed the structural pattern: Medicare Advantage plans deny 17% of initial claims, compared to 8% in traditional fee-for-service Medicare. When denied, most claims are reversed on appeal, yet provider reimbursements still dip 7% relative to traditional Medicare. The denial-then-reverse cycle is not a quality control mechanism. It is a float strategy: delay payment, reduce the percentage of patients who persist, and pocket the difference.

Game Theory Concept
Strategic Compliance: Transparency as Information Game

When regulators mandate disclosure, they assume that transparency disciplines behavior. But disclosure rules create a new strategic variable: how to aggregate. Insurers choosing to report approval rates across all service types (including bulk-auto-approved routine requests) rather than by clinical category can comply with the letter of CMS-0057-F while neutralizing its intent. This is a second-order information asymmetry: the raw data is public, but the framing is controlled. The AMA recognized this and pushed CMS for service-level and plan-level breakdowns, numeric counts alongside percentages, and inclusion of all utilization management processes that function as prior authorization. Until the reporting format forces disaggregation, the transparency rule is a mechanism that generates data without generating accountability. In game-theoretic terms, the regulator won the right to see the cards, but the insurer still controls which cards are face-up.

The Successor's Dilemma

Tim Noel, who succeeded Brian Thompson as UnitedHealthcare's CEO, has executed more structural PA reform in six months than the entire industry managed in the preceding decade. The game theory behind this is instructive.

Thompson could not reform. Admitting the system was broken would have been an admission of corporate liability and a gift to the class action plaintiffs. Every PA eliminated was a tacit concession that the PA it replaced was unnecessary. The incumbent was trapped in a commitment device of his own making: the denial infrastructure was the product, and dismantling it meant undermining the quarterly earnings narrative that kept his stock price at $600.

Noel inherited a different game. He was appointed after the crisis. Every reform he announces gets read as responsible stewardship, not a confession. Where Thompson would have been punished by the market for the same moves, Noel gets rewarded: the stock rallied from $260 to $400 on the back of his reform announcements and the Q2 earnings beat they enabled. In game theory terms, this is the successor advantage in sequential games: the second mover can make concessions that the first mover cannot, because the second mover is not carrying the reputational cost of having built the system being reformed.

The specificity of Noel's reforms is also significant. These are not vague pledges. He eliminated PA for 1,500 rural hospitals. He eliminated two-thirds of PA for children under 18. He named echocardiograms, outpatient surgeries, chiropractic care. He introduced authorization waivers at nationally recognized children's hospitals. Each announcement was a precise, verifiable commitment that could be tracked. In mechanism design terms, he replaced cheap talk (the 2018 and 2025 industry pledges, which delivered almost nothing) with costly signals: specific, measurable, and publicly embarrassing to reverse.

Game Theory Concept
Costly Signaling vs. Cheap Talk

Michael Spence's signaling theory (1973 Nobel) distinguishes between signals that are costly to fake and "cheap talk" that carries no commitment cost. The 2018 Consensus Statement was cheap talk: vague pledges with no enforcement. Noel's reforms are costly signals: specific, named services with verifiable elimination dates that would damage credibility if reversed. The market responded accordingly, pricing UNH's recovery at $400 per share. The transition from cheap talk to costly signaling is what separates performative reform from structural change.

The most telling data point: Noel disclosed that PA is required for "only 2% of UnitedHealthcare medical services," with 92% approved in under 24 hours. If that was true before the shooting, it raises an uncomfortable question: why was the system generating hundreds of thousands of denials for a process that applied to 2% of volume and approved nearly everything? The answer is that the 2% figure obscures the denominator. The 2% includes routine office visits and lab draws that never required PA. The services that do require PA, such as cancer treatment, specialty drugs, MRIs, and surgical procedures, are the ones where denial has the highest human cost. A 10% denial rate on cancer treatment is worse than a 50% denial rate on chiropractic visits, even if the aggregate "approval rate" looks identical.

The Buffett Trade: Capital as Coordination Signal

On August 15, 2025, Berkshire Hathaway's quarterly 13F filing disclosed a new position: 5 million shares of UnitedHealth Group, acquired during Q2 at roughly $271 per share, totaling $1.6 billion. UNH had lost more than half its market value since early 2025. Warren Buffett, who built his fortune on insurance companies and famously counseled buying "when others are fearful," had just placed the largest institutional bet that the post-Thompson crisis was overdone.

The stock surged 14% in a single day. Over the following months, it rallied 37%. Other hedge fund managers followed Berkshire in. By the time UNH reported its Q2 2026 earnings beat, the stock had recovered from $260 to $400.

Then Berkshire sold everything. The Q1 2026 13F, disclosed on May 15, 2026, showed zero UNH shares remaining. Berkshire had exited at approximately $394 per share, banking a 45% return in roughly nine months. Under new CEO Greg Abel, the rapid entry-exit was faster than Buffett's traditional long-term hold style. The market's reaction was mild: UNH fell only 2%, and analysts read the sale as profit-taking, not loss of conviction.

In game theory, Buffett's trade functioned as a coordination signal in an information-asymmetric market. After the Thompson shooting, institutional investors faced a collective action problem: UNH was undervalued relative to its fundamentals, but no single investor wanted to be the first to buy into a company associated with a national backlash against its entire business model. The risk was reputational as much as financial. When the most trusted name in institutional investing bought $1.6 billion of the stock, he solved the coordination problem. Other investors could follow without bearing the reputational cost of being the first mover into "the company whose CEO was assassinated for denying claims."

This is Thomas Schelling's focal point theory in action. In a coordination game where multiple equilibria exist (UNH is either a value trap or a recovery play), a credible signal from a high-reputation player collapses the uncertainty. Buffett did not need to be right about UNH's long-term prospects. He needed to be credible enough that other investors would follow, creating the very recovery his purchase predicted. The 14% single-day surge on the disclosure was not a response to new information about UNH's fundamentals. It was a coordination event: the market collectively deciding that the crisis discount was over because Buffett said so.

Game Theory Concept
Capital Markets as Enforcement Mechanism

The Buffett trade reveals how capital markets enforce reform through a feedback loop. The stock's collapse from $600 to $260 punished the old denial-heavy model. Buffett's buy at $271 priced in that reform was coming. Noel's verifiable PA reductions earned the stock's recovery to $400. Berkshire's exit at $394 closed the loop: the crisis discount was arbitraged away, and the new equilibrium was priced. Each step reinforced the next. Markets did not cause the reform, but they provided the scoreboard that made reform's financial payoff visible. This is a repeated game where the market acts as a mechanism designer: it rewards the strategies that reduce systemic risk and punishes the ones that created it. The self-fulfilling nature of Buffett's signal is the mechanism at work.

The Disparity Multiplier

The game theory of prior authorization carries an unexamined assumption: that the burden of denial falls randomly across the insured population. It does not. Recent evidence shows that PA functions as a disparity multiplier, concentrating its costs on patients who are least equipped to navigate the system.

A 2026 study in JACC: Advances tracked 2,183 heart failure patients at NYU Langone Health who received new prescriptions for two classes of lifesaving drugs: angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs) and SGLT2 inhibitors. Both are pillars of modern heart failure treatment with no generic alternatives. Patients whose prescriptions required prior authorization took three times as long to fill an ARNI prescription and six times as long to fill an SGLT2 inhibitor. Patients facing PA for SGLT2 inhibitors were 2.23 times more likely to never fill them at all. The study's lead author, Dr. Amrita Mukhopadhyay: "Our results suggest that prior authorization may be doing harm when it comes to guideline-recommended medications with no generic alternatives."

The disparity finding was stark. Prior authorization requirements were disproportionately imposed on patients who identified as Black or Hispanic, those living in lower socioeconomic-status neighborhoods, and those with Medicaid insurance. The researchers concluded that "these policies may be reinforcing health care disparities." The people least able to navigate a 12-hour-per-week bureaucracy are the ones most likely to face it.

A separate July 2026 study in JAMA Network Open (Thom et al., University of North Carolina) surveyed nearly 1,000 U.S. adults on their direct experience with prior authorization. Among patients who engaged directly with the PA process, 59% reported delays in care, 27% reported denials, and nearly a third rated the process a "moderate" or "serious" problem. Direct patient involvement in PA was associated with a 2.12-fold increased risk of care not being approved or received. Among caregivers, the numbers were worse: 76% reported delays and 27% reported the prescribed care was denied outright. The study is among the first to quantify caregiver burden, revealing that prior authorization does not just harm patients. It drafts their families into a bureaucratic fight that most are unequipped to win.

Game Theory Concept
Disparate Impact as Mechanism Design Flaw

The appeals mechanism was designed to correct wrongful denials. But the mechanism's cost structure (time, literacy, phone calls, employer engagement, legal consultation) selects for socioeconomic capacity, not medical need. In mechanism design terms, the correction channel has a participation cost that is inversely correlated with vulnerability: the patients most harmed by denial are least likely to appeal. When that cost structure intersects with racial and economic disparities in who faces PA requirements in the first place, the mechanism produces a systematic transfer of health risk from insurers to marginalized communities. This is not a side effect of the system. It is the system functioning exactly as its incentives predict.

For the statistical lives calculation, this disparity matters. If the 6.5 million eliminated PA requirements were distributed proportionally, then the lives saved are disproportionately concentrated among patients who would never have appealed, never have navigated the bureaucracy, and never have received the care their physicians prescribed. The conservative estimate of 500 to 1,500 lives, built on delay coefficients from well-resourced cancer centers, likely understates the mortality impact in populations with fewer resources to fight the system.

The Provider Revolt

The game theory presented so far treats this as a two-player contest: insurers versus patients (or their political proxies). But by mid-2026, a third player entered the game with institutional resources that individual patients lack. Hospital systems started suing.

In April 2026, Jefferson Health filed a federal lawsuit against Aetna challenging its "level of severity inpatient payment policy," which took effect January 1. The policy approves certain Medicare Advantage inpatient stays of one to four midnights but reimburses them at observation-level rates unless Aetna's internal severity criteria are met. Jefferson alleged the policy creates a payment tier that was never negotiated in its contract and violates CMS's two-midnight rule, which determines when a hospital stay should be billed as inpatient. The complaint cited a 72-year-old stroke patient requiring ICU care and intubation who was initially classified as "low severity." In game-theoretic terms, Aetna was defecting within a cooperative agreement: telling CMS and patients it "covered" the inpatient admission while paying the hospital an outpatient rate.

Three months later, on July 22, Jefferson sued Independence Blue Cross for nearly $100 million, alleging five separate reimbursement policy changes that breached its contract. The cumulative effect: Jefferson faced more than $24 million in denied or downgraded inpatient payments, plus $11.5 million from a policy that runs emergency admissions through InterQual criteria and pays the lower observation rate when the case "doesn't look severe enough." Jefferson was not alone. In May, the California Hospital Association sued Anthem Blue Cross on behalf of more than 400 member hospitals, challenging a policy that reduces facility reimbursement by 10% when an enrollee receives services from an out-of-network physician at an in-network hospital. Elevance Health had introduced the policy in multiple states.

The pattern extended beyond bilateral disputes. Provider antitrust litigation against Zelis and MultiPlan (now Claritev) alleged that insurers used shared repricing vendors to coordinate and suppress out-of-network payments. A Massachusetts federal judge denied motions to dismiss in March 2026, and the case moved to discovery. Arizona's attorney general filed a separate antitrust suit in June. The Capitol Forum reported that the Department of Justice was conducting a criminal price-fixing investigation, though Claritev stated it had not been told it was a target. Meanwhile, the $2.67 billion Blue Cross Blue Shield subscriber antitrust settlement from 2020 finally began distributing payments in 2026, while a separate $2.8 billion provider settlement, the largest healthcare antitrust settlement in U.S. history, reached final approval in August 2025. Nearly 6,500 providers opted out to pursue independent litigation.

Game Theory Concept
Three-Player Game: The Provider Coordination Problem

For decades, individual hospitals accepted insurer terms because defection was too costly: losing an insurer's network meant losing its patients. This is a classic hold-up problem. The post-Thompson environment changed three variables simultaneously. First, public sympathy shifted the political cost of siding with insurers. Second, regulatory momentum (OIG reports, CMS rules, state laws) gave providers legal ammunition that did not exist before. Third, hospital associations solved the collective action problem that individual hospitals could not: the CHA lawsuit covers 400+ hospitals, making network exclusion retaliatory rather than rational. When providers coordinate through associations, antitrust coalitions, and parallel filings, the game shifts from bilateral hold-up to multilateral pressure. The insurer's dominant strategy of unilateral policy changes collapses when the provider side can credibly threaten coordinated exit or litigation at scale.

The provider revolt also exposes a structural asymmetry in the denial game. When an insurer denies a patient, the patient bears the cost (delay, worsened health, out-of-pocket spending) and has limited recourse. When an insurer changes reimbursement policy to underpay a hospital, the hospital bears the cost but has litigation resources, contract law standing, and the ability to generate bad press. The Jefferson Health lawsuits are not about prior authorization per se. They are about the same underlying incentive structure: maximize retained premiums by minimizing payouts, whether to patients or providers. Providers filing suit are, in effect, enforcing the very accountability that patients cannot enforce individually. The game theory predicts this outcome: when the two-player equilibrium (insurer vs. patient) is unstable because one player has no enforcement power, a third player with enforcement power will eventually enter.

On August 5, 2026, a federal judge in Pennsylvania dealt the provider revolt its most significant procedural victory. Aetna had moved to compel arbitration in the Jefferson Health case and to exclude the AHA's amicus brief, seeking to keep the dispute confidential and bilateral. Judge John M. Gallagher denied both motions. "If the Policy directly affects AHA and HAP member hospitals, particularly by creating administrative and financial burdens, then Movants may properly take a special interest in the litigation's outcome," he wrote. "Their role as national trade associations strengthens, rather than weakens, that interest." The ruling's significance extends beyond this case. Aetna's arbitration strategy was designed to exploit exactly the coordination problem that individual providers face: in private arbitration, each hospital bears full legal costs alone, precedent does not accumulate, and public scrutiny is impossible. The AHA's entry as amicus converts a bilateral dispute into a test case for the entire hospital industry. If Jefferson Health prevails on the merits, the ruling will establish that unilateral mid-contract reimbursement changes violate the provider agreements that hundreds of hospitals signed. If Aetna prevails, it will embolden every insurer to replicate the "level of severity" policy. The judge's refusal to compel arbitration is itself a game-theoretic move: it keeps the game public, where reputational costs constrain insurer behavior, rather than private, where they do not.

The exit matters as much as the entry. By selling at $394, Berkshire signaled that UNH's "reform premium" had been fully captured. The remaining risks (DOJ investigation, Medicare Advantage margin pressure, elevated medical costs) were priced in. Future returns would come from operational execution, not crisis recovery. This is important for the game theory: when the crisis discount disappears, the market's leverage over the industry diminishes. The "threat" of further stock collapse, which pressured Noel into reform, attenuates once the stock stabilizes at its post-reform level. The ratchet holds (reforms cannot easily be reversed), but the urgency that drove the pace of reform may slow.

The Preemption Paradox: Federalism as a Chicken Game

The standard game-theoretic model of regulation assumes a single regulator disciplining a single industry. The American insurance reform landscape is structurally different: it is a multi-level game in which state legislatures, a deregulatory federal executive, bipartisan congressional coalitions, and the insurance industry are all playing simultaneously with different objectives, different information, and different time horizons. The result is a coordination problem that game theory calls a Chicken game, and its resolution will determine whether the post-Thompson reforms survive or get rolled back through federal preemption.

The four players and their strategies are distinct. State legislatures, at least eight of which have now enacted binding AI-in-insurance restrictions, are racing to establish regulatory facts on the ground before federal preemption can override them. The Trump administration's December 2025 executive order on AI explicitly threatens to restrict federal funding or litigate against states that enact "excessive" AI regulation, treating the question as one of national competitiveness rather than consumer protection. Congress, meanwhile, is advancing bipartisan reform bills with near-unanimous committee votes (298 House cosponsors for the Improving Seniors' Timely Access Act alone). And the insurance industry occupies an awkward position: publicly denying it uses AI for denials while privately facing lawsuits alleging the opposite.

The paradox crystallized in February 2026, when the House Ways and Means Committee hauled in executives from Cigna, UnitedHealth Group, and other major insurers to address concerns about affordability and algorithmic claims processing. Under oath, Cigna CEO David Cordani told lawmakers that AI is "never used for a denial." Other executives either denied or avoided discussing the use of advanced technology to reject authorization requests. These statements were made while Cigna was being sued for its PxDx system, which ProPublica had documented as enabling physicians to reject claims in batches without reading patient files. The hearing illustrates a signaling game in its purest form: the cost of admitting AI use (regulatory consequences, litigation exposure, public outrage) exceeds the cost of denial (potential perjury risk, media scrutiny), so executives choose the strategy of categorical denial regardless of underlying facts. The signal is strategic, not informative.

The more consequential game is between the states and the federal executive. In Chicken, two players drive toward each other; both prefer the other to swerve, but mutual collision is the worst outcome for both. Here, the collision scenario is federal litigation against popular, bipartisan state consumer protection laws. A December 2025 Fox News poll found that 63% of American voters describe themselves as "very" or "extremely" concerned about artificial intelligence, with majorities across Democrats, Republicans, and independents. When Florida Governor DeSantis, a former Republican presidential candidate, rolls out an "AI Bill of Rights" with insurance restrictions in the same legislative session that Democratic-led Maryland and Illinois enact similar bans, the political cost of federal preemption becomes extreme. No administration wants to be seen suing states for protecting patients from algorithmic denials, especially not when the public anger that followed Thompson's killing remains a live political force.

The game-theoretic prediction is that both sides will "swerve" by partitioning the regulatory domain. The McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945 established insurance as primarily a state-regulated industry, a legal precedent that gives states a structural advantage in any preemption fight involving insurance-specific AI restrictions. The most likely equilibrium is selective non-enforcement: the Trump EO applies to AI regulation generally (autonomous vehicles, content moderation, hiring algorithms) but stops short of overriding state insurance laws where bipartisan support is deepest and voter anger is sharpest. This is Nash equilibrium in a Chicken game: both sides avoid collision by claiming different lanes, with the insurance industry's regulatory obligations determined by whichever jurisdictional claim is more politically costly to challenge.

California's approach may set the practical standard. Effective January 2026, California law requires that any health insurer in the state must have a licensed physician or qualified healthcare provider review and decide on every denial, delay, or modification of care based on medical necessity. This is not a ban on AI in the review process; it is a requirement that a human being with medical credentials make the final determination. The distinction matters: it sidesteps the preemption question entirely, because it regulates the qualifications of decision-makers (a traditional state insurance function under McCarran-Ferguson) rather than regulating the technology itself. Other states are watching. If California's framing survives legal challenge, it provides a template that is functionally AI-resistant while being formally technology-neutral.

The scale of what is being contested is staggering. KFF analysis of CMS data found that Medicare Advantage insurers made nearly 53 million prior authorization determinations in 2024 alone, a volume that no system of purely human review could process in the timeframes now required by CMS-0057-F (72 hours for urgent requests, seven days for standard). The industry's dilemma is structural: AI and algorithmic processing are operationally necessary to handle the volume, but politically and legally toxic to acknowledge. This creates a new form of information asymmetry: insurers know they use algorithmic tools, regulators suspect it, patients experience its effects, but no party has an incentive to make the fact explicit. The congressional hearing, the public denials, and the state bans are all strategic moves in a game where the underlying truth is not in dispute; only its public acknowledgment is.

Game Theory Concept
Federalism Chicken: Regulatory Preemption as a Coordination Game

In a standard Chicken game, two players choose between "straight" (escalate) and "swerve" (concede). Both prefer the other to swerve, but mutual escalation is catastrophic. In the insurance AI regulation context, "straight" for the federal executive means enforcing the AI preemption EO against state insurance laws; "straight" for state legislatures means enacting laws specifically designed to resist preemption (e.g., framing AI restrictions as physician-qualification requirements rather than technology bans). The payoff structure makes mutual collision politically irrational: the federal government gains nothing from suing popular bipartisan laws in an election cycle, and states gain nothing from passing laws that get immediately enjoined. The equilibrium emerges through domain partitioning: the EO covers AI generally, state laws cover insurance specifically, and McCarran-Ferguson provides the legal firewall. The insurance industry, paradoxically, benefits from both: state compliance requirements provide political cover for reforms they would have resisted voluntarily, while the EO's general AI-friendly posture protects their non-insurance technology investments. The game produces more regulation than the federal executive intended and less than the state legislatures demanded, which is exactly the outcome Chicken predicts when both players swerve.

The Enforcement Gap: Penalties as Price Signals

In August 2026, CMS published its Contract Year 2025 audit and enforcement report, fining 14 Medicare sponsors a combined $1.54 million for 18 violations. CVS Health received the largest penalty at $753,805. Centene paid $380,785. UnitedHealth Group paid $48,869. In 89% of violations, enrollees suffered financial harm exceeding $100, including denial of access to drugs used for acute conditions requiring immediate treatment.

The audit's technical findings were more damning than the dollar amounts. CMS documented duplicate logic in a prior authorization system that failed after a software update, causing appeals to process incorrectly. It found approved coverage requests rendered unavailable for the rest of the plan year. It found patients denied transition-fill medications because the system checked prior enrollment dates instead of current ones. It found appeals submitted by a different prescriber restarted as brand-new coverage requests, resetting the decision clock. It found coverage decisions issued without reviewers having all relevant clinical information in front of them. These are not edge cases. They are systemic design failures in the infrastructure that determines who gets care.

The penalty arithmetic is revealing. UnitedHealth Group's $48,869 fine arrived in the same quarter that UNH reported $8 billion in operating earnings, growing 55% year over year. That penalty represents approximately 0.0006% of quarterly operating earnings, or roughly the equivalent of fining a household earning $80,000 a year about 48 cents. For CVS, the $753,805 was larger but still trivial against $13.8 billion in quarterly revenue. When the penalty for denying patients entitled care is less than the cost of preventing the denial, the rational actor treats the fine as an operating expense. Gary Becker's 1968 framework on crime and punishment predicts exactly this outcome: a penalty must exceed the expected profit from the violation, adjusted for the probability of detection, to alter behavior.

Game Theory Concept
Subgame Deterrence Failure: Penalties Below the Indifference Threshold

In a repeated game between a regulator and a regulated firm, enforcement functions as a deterrent only when the expected penalty exceeds the expected benefit of non-compliance. The expected penalty equals the fine amount multiplied by the probability of detection. With 18 violations across 14 sponsors covering tens of millions of enrollees, the detection rate per violation is vanishingly small. A $48,869 penalty for systematic failures affecting a covered population of 8+ million enrollees means the expected cost per incorrectly denied claim is measured in fractions of a cent. Becker (1968) would classify this as a system in which the "price" of non-compliance is set below the indifference threshold: the firm is economically indifferent between compliance and violation because the penalty cannot alter the payoff calculation. The Interoperability Rule's transparency requirements (public denial-rate reporting starting 2027) attempt to add a reputational penalty that monetary fines alone cannot provide. But whether transparency shifts the payoff matrix depends on whether consumers can act on the information, which brings the game full circle: most Medicare Advantage enrollees chose their plan during a narrow enrollment window and cannot easily switch.

The report's timing is pointed. It arrived five months before the January 1, 2027 deadline requiring all Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP, and marketplace plans to support electronic prior authorization through standardized APIs. Starting in 2027, payers must also publish annual prior authorization metrics on their websites: the share of requests approved, denied, and approved on appeal, plus the average time from submission to decision. CMS has signaled that compliance with the new timeframes is now an audit target. The combination of a transparency mandate with trivial financial penalties creates a distinctive enforcement regime: the regulator is betting that public shame will succeed where fines have failed. Whether that bet pays off depends on whether the new data empowers the other players in the game (patients, providers, employers, state attorneys general) to impose costs the regulator cannot.

The Regulator's Dilemma: When the Government Becomes the Denier

The WISeR Model deserves its own game-theoretic analysis because it violates a foundational assumption of mechanism design: that the regulator sits outside the game. CMS is simultaneously the entity fining private insurers for PA system failures and the entity running its own AI-driven PA system that denies care at higher rates than the private insurers it regulates.

The early data is damning. In Texas, where Cohere Health runs WISeR's prior authorization reviews, only 62% of requests received initial approval. After a physician review of the non-authorizations, the rate climbed to 84%. For comparison, the national Medicare Advantage approval rate exceeds 92%. Traditional Medicare, which WISeR targets, has historically operated without prior authorization for most services. CMS introduced a denial mechanism into a program that functioned without one, outsourced the denials to private vendors compensated based on a share of the "savings" they generate, and then faced a federal FOIA lawsuit for refusing to disclose how the AI algorithms make their decisions.

The vendor incentive structure is the critical game-theoretic flaw. WISeR's six contracted companies (Cohere Health, Genzeon, Humata Health, Innovaccer, Virtix Health, Zyter) receive compensation tied to the "savings" their reviews produce. The more procedures denied or delayed, the more revenue the vendors earn. This is the exact shared-savings structure that CMS criticizes when private insurers use it. When CMS fines UnitedHealth $48,869 for systematic PA failures while its own WISeR vendors operate under incentives that reward denial volume, the regulatory signal collapses. The referee has entered the game on the same side as the player it is supposed to be policing.

Game Theory Concept
The Regulator's Paradox: Credibility Collapse in Multi-Role Games

In classical mechanism design (Hurwicz 1960, Myerson 1981), the designer is assumed to be a disinterested party who sets rules but does not participate in the game those rules govern. When the designer becomes a player, two problems emerge. First, the designer's rules lose credibility: a regulator that profits from denials cannot credibly punish other firms for denials. Second, the designer gains private information about the game's mechanics that creates asymmetric advantage over other players and over the public. The EFF's FOIA lawsuit targets this second problem directly: CMS refused to disclose the training data, bias safeguards, or decision logic of WISeR's AI tools, replicating the same opacity that provoked the NaviHealth class action against UnitedHealth. When the regulator's opacity mirrors the regulated firm's opacity, the regulated firm's defense ("we are acting within the rules") becomes unfalsifiable. The GAO's May 12, 2026 determination that WISeR constitutes a "rule" under the Administrative Procedure Act introduces a constitutional meta-game: the Congressional Review Act allows Congress to void the executive branch's rule through an expedited process immune to Senate filibuster, a mechanism used fewer than 20 times since 1996.

The Congressional response was unusually coordinated. On May 20, 2026, Senator Ron Wyden and Representatives Suzan DelBene and Greg Landsman introduced a joint resolution of disapproval under the CRA to repeal WISeR entirely. The resolution's legal basis rested on the GAO's determination eight days earlier that WISeR meets the APA definition of a rule because it prescribes new requirements for Traditional Medicare providers. CMS had not submitted WISeR to Congress as a rule, itself a procedural violation that triggers the CRA's special procedures.

On July 16, 2026, the Senate voted 46-50 along strict party lines to reject the CRA resolution. Every Democrat voted to block WISeR; every Republican voted to let it continue. The result exposed a stark asymmetry: the bipartisan consensus that animates state AI-denial bans (DeSantis's "AI Bill of Rights" alongside blue-state legislation) and House-level PA reform (the Improving Seniors Act passed committee 42-0) collapses at the federal level when the instrument is a government-run program rather than a private insurer practice. WISeR survives not because Congress supports AI-powered denials, but because the CRA mechanism makes blocking a government program a party-line vote on executive authority. The program's architects may have miscalculated the breadth of opposition to algorithmic denial, but they correctly calculated that partisan discipline would hold on a procedural instrument.

CMS had already blinked once before the vote. In April 2026, it delayed WISeR implementation for two services (Deep Brain Stimulation for Parkinson's and Percutaneous Image-Guided Lumbar Decompression for spinal stenosis) "to allow additional time for operational readiness." The delay was a signaling game: CMS retreated on the most medically consequential services while maintaining the program's overall structure. After the Senate's party-line protection, CMS faces no immediate legislative threat, and the six WISeR states will continue to serve as a live experiment in government-administered algorithmic denial.

The Medicaid Squeeze: Contraction as Equilibrium

While the post-shooting reform narrative focuses on loosening prior authorization for the insured, a parallel development is shrinking the insured population itself. UnitedHealthcare is strategically shedding Medicare Advantage members: 7.6 million as of June 30, 2026, down from 8.4 million a year earlier. The company has exited 109 counties, shuttered over 100 plans covering roughly 600,000 members, and revised its projected 2026 MA enrollment loss from 1.3-1.4 million to 1.1 million. CEO Tim Noel framed this as "favoring margin recovery." The medical cost ratio improved from 89.4% to 86.7% in Q2 2026, and operating earnings surged 55% year-over-year to $8 billion. The company expects MA margins to exceed 3% by year's end. The message is clear: the insurer can be more profitable with fewer, healthier members.

The federal government is executing the same contraction at a larger scale. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, cuts $990 billion from Medicaid over the next decade and imposes, for the first time in the program's 60-year history, mandatory work requirements on Medicaid expansion enrollees. Adults aged 19 to 64 must document 80 hours per month of employment, job training, volunteering, or education to maintain coverage. Nebraska began enforcement on May 1, 2026. Montana and Arkansas followed on July 1. Iowa is set for December 1. Full national enforcement begins January 1, 2027. The Congressional Budget Office projects 5.2 million adults will lose Medicaid coverage by 2034. Georgetown's Center for Children and Families estimates 6 million from work requirements alone and 10 million total when combined with other coverage provisions. On June 29, 2026, a coalition of 25 states and the District of Columbia sued in Boston federal court to block the CMS implementation rule, arguing it narrows medical exemptions beyond what Congress authorized.

Game Theory Concept
Population Contraction as Equilibrium Selection

In a repeated game with heterogeneous players, one resolution to an unsustainable equilibrium is population contraction: the dominant player restructures the game by excluding the participants whose presence makes the payoff structure untenable. UNH's county exits and the Medicaid work requirements operate on the same principle. The insurer sheds the costliest members (those in unprofitable PPO markets with high utilization). The government sheds the poorest members (those who cannot navigate documentation requirements). Both moves improve the remaining players' payoff ratios without changing the game's rules. The reforms that followed the Thompson shooting are real, but they apply to a shrinking population. The prior authorization improvements benefit the 257 million who remain covered. The 5-10 million projected to lose Medicaid coverage enter a state with no prior authorization process to reform because they have no coverage at all. The equilibrium is "better rules, fewer players," which is stable precisely because the excluded players have even less political power than the prior authorization victims who preceded them.

The data on who loses coverage exposes the mechanism. Health policy researchers consistently find that most Medicaid enrollees subject to work requirements are already working. The coverage losses come not from idleness but from documentation barriers: gig workers without HR departments, caregivers whose exemptions require physician documentation, and people who simply do not receive the paperwork in time. An Annals of Internal Medicine study estimates that if work requirements expanded to all beneficiaries nationwide, 8.3 million adults could be disenrolled. Many would fall into a coverage gap: too impaired to meet reporting requirements but not disabled enough to qualify for exemptions. This mirrors the prior authorization problem in miniature. The barrier is not clinical. It is administrative. And the administrative burden falls disproportionately on the populations least equipped to navigate it.

The game-theoretic irony is structural. The Thompson shooting forced insurers to reduce administrative barriers to care for the insured. The OBBBA simultaneously erected new administrative barriers to being insured at all. The insurer's response to public rage about denial was to deny fewer claims. The government's response was to deny coverage entirely to millions of people who never file claims because they never get enrolled. Both moves reduce the system's exposure to the dynamic that produced December 4, 2024. But they do so by different mechanisms: the insurer softens the rules within the game; the government removes players from it.

The Credibility Gap: Voluntary Pledges as Cheap Talk

On June 25, 2025, the Trump administration convened a news conference where CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz stood alongside health insurance executives to announce a six-part voluntary pledge. Nearly 60 insurers signed commitments to reduce prior authorization volume, speed up decisions, honor 90-day continuity-of-care transitions, and adopt standardized electronic submission by January 1, 2027. Oz explicitly cited the Thompson shooting as the catalyst. "There's violence in the streets over these issues," he said. One year later, KFF Health News published its assessment: the pledge is fracturing. Eight of the original signatories refused to sign the April 2026 technology update that operationalized one of the six commitments. Half of the insurers contacted by KFF could not provide specifics about which services they had exempted from prior authorization. The Trump administration promised "public dashboards" to track compliance. No dashboards exist. HHS did not respond to KFF's questions about enforcement.

The data the industry did release tells its own story. AHIP announced 6.5 million fewer prior authorizations, an 11% reduction, but declined to break out which services were dropped or how reductions varied by insurer. Patient advocates called this statistic "hiding a lot of nuance," noting that the costliest services, including cancer treatment, appear untouched. Physicians reported that insurers are finding new friction points. Rep. Greg Murphy (R-NC), a physician and co-chair of the GOP Doctors Caucus who participated in the original pledge announcement, said the industry commitment has "no teeth." He told KFF he has "zero faith" in self-regulation: "They craft the pathways to basically deny things immediately with the hope that people will give up." When a Republican physician in Congress who stood at the podium with the insurers calls their pledge toothless, the credibility collapse is bipartisan.

A particularly revealing mechanism has emerged: retroactive denials. The pledge promised to honor existing authorizations during plan transitions, but says nothing about denying payment after care has already been approved and delivered. KFF documented patients whose insurers approved treatment, then reversed payment afterward. One insurer's authorization letters carried the clause "authorization is not a guarantee of claim payment." This is not a loophole. It is a unilateral contract modification that preserves the appearance of compliance while maintaining the revenue model. The insurer can report fewer prior authorization requirements while shifting denial to the post-hoc payment stage, where the patient has already incurred the debt and the provider has already delivered the care.

Game Theory Concept
Cheap Talk vs. Binding Commitment (Crawford & Sobel, 1982)

In signaling games, a message is "cheap talk" when it costs the sender nothing to make and nothing to break. Crawford and Sobel (1982) proved that cheap talk can convey information only when the sender's and receiver's interests are sufficiently aligned. When interests diverge, the equilibrium degrades: the receiver discounts the message, the sender inflates it, and the resulting equilibrium involves less information transmission than either side would prefer. The June 2025 pledge is a textbook case. The sender (insurers) faces no financial penalty for non-compliance. Eight signatories withdrew from the technology commitment with no consequence. No federal enforcement mechanism exists. No public dashboard materialized. The receiver (patients, Congress, the public) is rationally updating: Murphy's "zero faith" is the equilibrium response to a cheap-talk signal from a sender whose interests are misaligned. The bipartisan Senate investigation that followed is the institutional correction: converting cheap talk into a binding commitment game by introducing the threat of subpoena power and legislative consequences for non-compliance. The game theory predicts that voluntary pledges will produce real reform only when the cost of defection exceeds the cost of compliance. The current pledge fails that test.

Congress appears to agree. On July 14, 2026, Senators Richard Blumenthal (D-CT) and Josh Hawley (R-MO), members of the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, sent bipartisan letters to UnitedHealthcare, Humana, and CVS Health demanding internal records on how each insurer makes post-acute care coverage decisions, including any use of algorithms, software, or AI to determine medical necessity. The senators requested an inventory of predictive technologies, details on staffing, and data on how often skilled nursing stays are shortened through unilateral Medicare non-coverage notices. Blumenthal and Hawley's letter to Tim Noel cited the June 2026 OIG reports as evidence that the pledge has not worked: "The data contained in these reports indicate that the disparities the Subcommittee revealed either endure or have grown worse." The Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations has subpoena authority. This is the difference between cheap talk and binding commitment: the game changes when a player who can compel document production enters.

The timing matters. The Blumenthal-Hawley letters went out the same week the House Ways and Means Committee unanimously passed the Improving Seniors' Timely Access to Care Act by 42-0. The pledge was the industry's attempt to forestall legislation. The investigation is Congress's response to the pledge's failure. The game theory trajectory is clear: voluntary commitment collapses, oversight escalates, and the legislative pipeline fills. CMS's own former Deputy Administrator Chris Klomp acknowledged at the original pledge event that more than 50% of prior authorizations are still processed by phone or fax machine. The technology commitment was supposed to fix that by January 2027. With eight insurers already out, Georgetown professor Sabrina Corlette's assessment applies: "In the absence of clear rules, policies, standards, and mandates, insurance companies are going to do what makes sense for them to do financially."

The Vertical Integration Gambit: Ownership as Exit Strategy

While the industry pledged reform on prior authorization, UnitedHealth was executing a parallel strategy: if you cannot profit from denying claims, profit from providing the care. In June 2023, UNH's Optum subsidiary agreed to acquire Amedisys, one of the largest home health and hospice providers in the United States, for $3.3 billion in cash. This followed Optum's $5.4 billion acquisition of LHC Group, another major home health provider, which closed in February 2023. Combined, the two deals represent $8.7 billion invested in owning the care delivery infrastructure that UNH's insurance arm pays for. The game theory is straightforward: vertical integration hedges against regulatory risk. If PA reform reduces denial revenue (fewer denials = less cost avoidance), owning the delivery side captures the revenue that would otherwise go to independent providers. The insurer that pays the claim and the provider that receives it become the same corporate entity.

The DOJ recognized the anticompetitive implications but, under a business-friendly administration, opted for remedy over prohibition. In November 2024, the DOJ and four state attorneys general (Maryland, Illinois, New Jersey, New York) sued to block the Amedisys acquisition, arguing it was "presumptively anticompetitive and illegal." UNH already controlled a substantial home health and hospice network through LHC Group; adding Amedisys's nearly 500 locations across 32 states would give UNH 30% or more market share in eight states. In August 2025, the parties reached a settlement. By December 2025, the court entered its Final Judgment: UNH and Amedisys must divest at least 164 home health and hospice locations across 19 states, accounting for $528 million in annual revenue. BrightSpring Health Services (115 locations) and Pennant Group (49 locations) would acquire the divested assets. It was the largest divestiture of outpatient healthcare services to resolve a merger challenge in U.S. history. A court-appointed monitor would oversee compliance.

Two details illuminate the enforcement dynamic. First, Amedisys paid a $1.1 million civil penalty for "falsely certifying" that it had provided "true, correct, and complete" responses to DOJ document requests under the Hart-Scott-Rodino Act. The company literally lied to federal antitrust regulators during the review process and paid a penalty equal to 0.03% of the deal value. This echoes the Enforcement Gap pattern: when the fine is a rounding error on the transaction, the penalty functions as a toll, not a deterrent. Second, a group of Democratic lawmakers released a statement after the settlement calling it inadequate for protecting competition. Their concern is structural: divesting 164 of nearly 500 locations leaves UNH as potentially the largest home health and hospice provider in the United States, with vertically integrated control over insurance, pharmacy benefits (Optum Rx), physician practices (Optum Health), data analytics (Optum Insight), and now home-based care delivery.

Game Theory Concept
Vertical Integration as Risk Hedge (Williamson, 1975)

Oliver Williamson's transaction cost economics framework predicts that firms will vertically integrate when the cost of market transactions (including regulatory uncertainty, opportunism, and asset specificity) exceeds the cost of internal coordination. UNH's $8.7 billion investment in home health ownership is a textbook application: as regulatory pressure makes the denial-based insurance model riskier, bringing care delivery in-house reduces exposure to external providers who might ally with regulators against the insurer. But Williamson also identified the countervailing risk: vertical integration creates new conflicts of interest. An insurer that owns care delivery has incentive to channel patients to its own providers (favorable internal transfer pricing) rather than competitors (external market pricing). The DOJ's divestiture remedy attempts to preserve market competition in the overlap areas, but the structural incentive remains. The game theory prediction: as PA reform progresses, watch for patterns where UNH-insured patients are steered toward Optum-owned providers at rates that exceed what independent market dynamics would produce. The vertical integration gambit does not replace the denial model. It supplements it with an ownership model that generates revenue regardless of whether claims are approved or denied.

The acquisition's timing is itself a signal. The Amedisys deal was agreed in June 2023, when the class action alleging algorithmic denials was already filed and congressional scrutiny was building. The deal closed after the Thompson shooting, after the stock crash, after the CEO resignation, and after the voluntary pledge. UNH pursued vertical integration not despite the regulatory pressure but because of it. In a game where the insurance model's profitability is being squeezed from every direction (PA reform, disclosure mandates, state AI bans, provider lawsuits, federal audits), controlling the care delivery pipeline is the strategic fallback. The market appears to agree: UNH's stock recovery from $260 to $400 coincided with the Amedisys settlement's resolution, as investors priced in a company that can generate returns from care delivery even if the era of aggressive claim denial is ending.

But the vertical integration hedge is itself now under legislative assault. On February 10, 2026, Senators Elizabeth Warren (D-MA) and Josh Hawley (R-MO) introduced the Break Up Big Medicine Act, bipartisan legislation that would make it unlawful for any entity to simultaneously own an insurer or pharmacy benefit manager and a medical provider or management services organization. The bill directly targets UNH's corporate structure: Optum Health (90,000+ physicians), Optum Rx (PBM), and UnitedHealthcare (insurer) could not coexist under the same parent. Violators would face automatic penalties including profit disgorgement and forced asset sales, with a one-year divestiture deadline. Enforcement authority is distributed across the FTC, DOJ, HHS, state attorneys general, and private parties. Mark Cuban endorsed the legislation as part of his broader "Break Up Big Medicine" campaign, arguing that 80% of physicians now work for a corporate parent and that 4,000 independent pharmacies have closed since 2019 because vertically integrated conglomerates steer business to their own affiliates.

The game theory is now a two-front war. The denial model faces PA reform, disclosure mandates, and state AI bans from one direction. The vertical integration fallback, supposed to hedge against those reforms, faces structural separation legislation from the other. Williamson's framework predicted that firms integrate when market transaction costs exceed internal coordination costs. The Break Up Big Medicine Act inverts the calculus: it makes internal coordination illegal, forcing the market transaction costs that vertical integration was designed to avoid. If the bill passes, UNH's $8.7 billion investment in care delivery infrastructure becomes not a hedge but a liability requiring forced divestiture within 12 months. The Jones Day analysis flagged an additional risk: the bill's text does not define "insurance company" (while defining "health plans"), potentially capturing integrated health systems and physician groups with subsidiary health plans beyond the conglomerates the sponsors intend to target. Warren and Hawley's bipartisan sponsorship is itself a signal. When the Senate's most prominent progressive and its most prominent populist conservative agree that vertical integration is the problem, the political viability of structural separation has shifted from theoretical to plausible.

The Patient Counter-Attack: How AI and Social Media Collapsed the Appeal Barrier

While legislators attacked the supply side of denials, a parallel shift was collapsing the demand-side barrier that made denials profitable: the prohibitive cost of appealing. The 0.2% appeal rate that underpinned the entire denial calculus was not a reflection of patient indifference. It was a function of information asymmetry and transaction costs. Patients lacked the medical coding knowledge, legal vocabulary, and bureaucratic stamina to navigate a system designed to exhaust them. That asymmetry is dissolving.

Claimable, an AI startup founded by Warris Bokhari, drafts insurance appeal letters on behalf of patients using large language models trained on denial patterns, medical coding standards, and successful appeal strategies. The service gained national attention in August 2026 when MarketWatch reported on Payton Herres, a 26-year-old heart transplant recipient whose insurer, Elevance Health, denied coverage of her anti-rejection medication. After Herres posted her story on Facebook, Cuban amplified it on LinkedIn, and Claimable's nonprofit arm covered the cost of her medication through Cost Plus Drugs at $300 for 90 days, a fraction of the $1,000 co-pay Elevance had set. Elevance later acknowledged the denial "did not fully account for Ms. Herres' treatment history." The Herres case illustrates a new equilibrium: when the cost of generating a credible appeal approaches zero (AI drafts the letter), and the cost of public shaming approaches infinity (a CEO's LinkedIn post reaches millions), the expected-value calculation that made denial profitable inverts.

Cuban's role is structurally interesting. His Cost Plus Drugs pharmacy, which sells medications at cost plus a 15% markup and a small dispensing fee, bypasses the PBM layer entirely. His congressional testimony accused PBMs of preferring higher drug list prices because their rebates and fees scale with the wholesale acquisition cost. His social media advocacy turns individual denial stories into collective pressure. And his August 17 post about the 61% universal owner problem connected the investment thesis to the moral thesis in a single sentence. He is not a patient, a physician, an insurer, or a regulator. He is a new player: the amplifier, whose game-theoretic function is to collapse the information asymmetry that previously shielded denial decisions from public scrutiny. When Cuban asks "How could they not tell the CEO that for 18K a month they could save the leg of a child?", he is converting a private denial into a public accountability event. The insurer's optimal strategy in a world without amplifiers (deny, bet on exhaustion) becomes suboptimal in a world with them (deny, face viral exposure, reverse under pressure, absorb reputational damage).

Game Theory Concept
The Transparency Weapon: Information Revelation and Equilibrium Shift (Milgrom, 1981)

Paul Milgrom's unraveling result demonstrates that in markets where some sellers can credibly disclose quality, all sellers eventually must, because silence is interpreted as concealment. Applied to insurance denials: when AI tools make it trivially cheap for some patients to generate credible appeals, and social media makes those appeals publicly visible, the information asymmetry that sustained the denial model unravels. Each successful public appeal reveals the denial's weakness. Each revealed weakness makes the next denial harder to defend. The 0.2% appeal rate was an equilibrium sustained by high transaction costs. AI-drafted appeals and social media amplification are lowering those costs toward zero. Milgrom's prediction: as appeal costs fall, the denial rate must converge to the clinically defensible rate, because every marginal denial above that rate faces an increasing probability of public exposure and reversal. The insurer's dominant strategy shifts from "deny and bet on exhaustion" to "approve preemptively to avoid the reputational cost of reversal." This is not a prediction. It is already happening: UNH's pediatric PA elimination (two-thirds of services for members under 18), announced in May 2026, is the corporate acknowledgment that denial of routine care is no longer worth the expected cost of public exposure.

The Coordination Miracle: How $15 Solved Olson's Problem

Mancur Olson's most durable prediction is that large, diffuse groups cannot organize. The benefits of collective action are public goods: if the health insurance system improves, everyone benefits regardless of whether they contributed. Rational actors free-ride. Small, concentrated groups (insurers, hospital systems, pharma) organize because the per-member stakes justify the coordination cost. Patients, numbering in the hundreds of millions with individually small grievances spread across thousands of conditions and dozens of insurers, remain atomized. This asymmetry was the insurance industry's structural advantage for decades. Patients could not coordinate because coordination was not rational.

The Thompson shooting changed the math. As of August 2026, more than 40,000 individual donors have contributed over $1.6 million to Mangione's legal defense fund through GiveSendGo, nearing the stated goal of $1.75 million. The median donation is $15. Six thousand letters have arrived at the Metropolitan Detention Center from 66 countries, with a handwritten log numbering 82 pages. A volunteer team of 30 people, calling themselves Stats4Lulu, maintains a public dashboard tracking donations, media coverage, and correspondence. When Mangione pled guilty on August 14, 2026 and told the court "I knew what I was doing was illegal," another 177 donors contributed $5,300 over the following weekend. The five most common words in donor comments are: free, people, love, support, hope.

None of this is rational legal investment. Mangione comes from a prominent Maryland real estate family. His legal representation, led by Karen Friedman Agnifilo and Marc Agnifilo, was never in financial jeopardy. A $15 donation to a defense fund that has already surpassed $1.6 million does not change the legal outcome. But that is precisely the point. The donation is not a purchase. It is a signal. Each $15 contribution is a costly signal of membership in a coalition that did not exist before December 4, 2024.

Game Theory Concept
Focal Point Coordination and the Dissolution of Free-Riding (Schelling, 1960; Olson, 1965)

Olson's collective action problem assumes that coordination costs exceed the individual benefit of participation. But Thomas Schelling demonstrated that salient events can serve as focal points that collapse coordination barriers without any central organizer. The Thompson shooting functioned as a Schelling focal point for a latent class: hundreds of millions of people who had experienced prior authorization denials, claim delays, or treatment abandonment but had no mechanism for collective expression. The shooting did not create the grievance. It created the coordination device. The defense fund, the letters, and the volunteer infrastructure are not organizations in the traditional sense. They are self-assembling coordination equilibria: each participant joins because they observe others joining, and the act of joining makes the coalition more visible, which attracts more participants. This is the opposite of Olson's prediction. When the focal point is salient enough and the participation cost is low enough ($15), the free-riding incentive collapses. A $15 donation is cheap enough that the signaling value exceeds the cost, and the signal is public enough (donor comments are visible) that coordination becomes self-reinforcing. The health insurance industry's structural advantage, patient atomization, dissolved in a weekend.

The industry's response confirmed the coordination threat. UnitedHealthcare's investor conference, the event Thompson was attending when he was killed, was not open to patients. It was attended by "company executives, the board of directors, and hundreds of investors, not doctors, nurses, and patients," as Mangione noted in his allocution. But the defense fund created a parallel investor conference of sorts: 40,000 people committing capital to signal that patient grievances are now organized and visible. The anonymous jury order for the state trial, requested because prosecutors raised concerns about "fan intimidation," acknowledged that the coordination extended beyond symbolic donations to potential real-world influence on legal proceedings.

The numbers tell a story about the depth of the coordination. The largest single donation was $36,500, from an anonymous donor who cited "the denial of equal justice under law" as "class-driven." The second-largest was $30,000, timed to Mangione's birthday. But 28,000 of the 40,000 contributions were under $27. This distribution, a tiny number of large donors and a vast base of small ones, matches the structure of a political movement, not a legal defense fund. For comparison, the median contribution to Bernie Sanders' 2020 presidential campaign was $18. The defense fund's $15 median places it in the same category: mass participation driven by ideological alignment, not financial optimization.

Manhattan DA Alvin Bragg identified the tension directly: "Celebrating murder is abhorrent. I sit across the table from families who've had a loved one killed. And to think of people celebrating that is beyond comprehension to me." NYPD Commissioner Tisch's post-plea statement, "A killer is not a hero," was an explicit attempt to degrade the focal point, to reassert the framing that the act was criminal rather than political. That both the DA and the police commissioner felt compelled to address the coordination, rather than just the crime, reveals the game theory: the prosecution is not merely litigating a murder case. It is competing for narrative control over a coordination device that has reshaped public discourse about health insurance.

The pre-shooting equilibrium relied on patient grievances remaining diffuse, individual, and invisible. A patient denied coverage might call their insurer, file an appeal, or in 0.2% of cases, pursue external review. These actions were isolated, unobserved by other patients, and structurally incapable of generating collective pressure. The defense fund and its infrastructure represent the dissolution of that isolation. Whether the coordination persists beyond the sentencing depends on whether the focal point retains its salience. The December 18 sentencing hearing, where Mangione's attorneys will present mitigation evidence and the court will determine a sentence between 24 years and life, provides the next coordination event. If the state murder charges are dismissed on double jeopardy grounds, simplifying the narrative to a single resolved proceeding, the focal point may actually strengthen: a clean story is easier to coordinate around than an ambiguous one.

The Confession Premium: Why Admitting Guilt Increased Support

Standard deterrence theory predicts that a guilty plea should collapse public support. When a defendant confesses, the sympathetic narrative of "he might be innocent" disappears. The donation incentive shifts: legal defense funds exist to fund a defense, and a guilty plea eliminates the trial. Yet the opposite happened. In the 72 hours following Mangione's August 14 guilty plea, 177 donors contributed $5,300 to his defense fund through the December 4 Legal Committee. "Luigi, I am so sorry about today," wrote one donor who gave $25. "We love you and will not stop supporting you. Stay strong." Another wrote: "Luigi is not the one who should go to prison. The healthcare system has victimised millions more people." The fund, which had plateaued for months, resumed growth toward its new $1.75 million goal.

This is not irrational behavior. It is a revealed preference about the nature of the coordination. The donations were never about funding a legal defense. They were about declaring membership in a political movement. Mangione's confession did not weaken the focal point because the focal point was never "he might be innocent." It was "the system deserved it." His statement in court, "I knew what I was doing was illegal," did not contradict the donor narrative. It confirmed it. He did it on purpose, accepted the consequences, and the system he targeted has since been forced to eliminate 6.5 million prior authorizations. The confession transformed him from an accused killer into a self-declared one, and for a supporter base that views the health insurance system as the real criminal, the distinction is morally irrelevant.

The game theory here involves Akerlof's concept of identity utility. Standard rational choice assumes preferences are fixed and exogenous. But identity economics recognizes that individuals derive utility from acting in accordance with their self-concept. A donor who identifies as "someone who fights the healthcare system" gains utility from the $15 donation that has nothing to do with the legal outcome. The guilty plea actually increases the identity payoff: before the confession, donating was ambiguous (supporting a potentially innocent person, which any liberal might do). After the confession, donating is unambiguous (supporting a self-confessed killer because you believe the cause was just). The act of donating post-confession is a stronger identity signal precisely because it is more socially costly. It is the costly signaling model applied to political identity: the higher the social cost of the signal, the more credible it is as a marker of genuine commitment.

Kenneth Belkin, a New York criminal defense attorney, articulated the opposing view directly: "Pretty egregious that some people are still donating to his legal defence as if he didn't just admit on the record in court to a cold-blooded killing. It's not like he's some indigent defendant who may plausibly be innocent." Belkin's framing assumes the legal defense rationale was ever the primary motivation. The post-plea donation data proves it was not. The fund is operating as a political action committee disguised as a legal defense fund, and the guilty plea stripped away the pretense without reducing the underlying demand for the signal.

The 82-page handwritten log of mail Mangione has received, maintained by the Metropolitan Detention Center, documents the same phenomenon in analog form. Ten to 115 letters arrive daily. The volume did not decrease after the plea. In game-theoretic terms, the confession functioned as a separating equilibrium: it sorted the support base into those who were motivated by "he might be innocent" (who left) and those motivated by "the system is the real criminal" (who stayed and intensified). The remaining coalition is smaller but more committed, a pattern consistent with radicalization dynamics in social movements. For the insurance industry, this is the more threatening outcome. A broad but shallow support base dissipates quickly. A narrow but deep one persists and can be mobilized for the next event: the December 18 sentencing, the state double jeopardy ruling, or the next health insurance executive who appears at a public event.

The Attrition Shield: How Insurers Win by Not Losing

The game theory of health insurance reform assumes that legal and regulatory mechanisms can impose costs on insurers that exceed the benefits of denial. But this assumption ignores a structural asymmetry: insurers do not need to win their legal battles. They need to outlast them. The data from 2026 shows this strategy operating across three simultaneous fronts, and succeeding on all of them.

Front one: the arbitration compulsion. On March 6, 2026, U.S. District Judge Brian Murphy granted motions from Aetna, Elevance Health, and UnitedHealth Group to compel individual arbitration in the Zelis antitrust class action. The ruling forced providers who had signed contracts with arbitration clauses out of the class proceeding and into individual disputes. "The court's hands are tied," Judge Murphy acknowledged, recognizing that the contractual clauses overrode the providers' collective litigation strategy regardless of the underlying antitrust merits. Three weeks later, Judge Murphy denied the joint motion to dismiss the remaining claims, allowing the Zelis case to proceed for non-arbitrating plaintiffs. But the damage was done: the class had been fractured. Providers who arbitrate individually bear full legal costs against insurers with standing outside counsel. This is Olson's collective action problem weaponized. The very mechanism that makes class actions effective for diffuse, low-stakes plaintiffs (cost pooling) is neutralized by the arbitration clause that each provider signed when they joined the network.

Game Theory Concept: War of Attrition / Procedural Exhaustion as Strategic Moat

In Maynard Smith and Price's war of attrition model (1973), two competitors display without fighting, and the one willing to persist longer wins the resource. The optimal strategy depends on the asymmetry of war chests: the player with lower cost-of-waiting always wins in equilibrium. UnitedHealth Group, with $450 billion in annual revenue and a permanent legal infrastructure, has a near-zero marginal cost of adding another year to any proceeding. Individual providers, whistleblowers, and budget-constrained federal agencies do not. The arbitration compulsion, prolonged litigation, and parallel-front strategy are not defensive tactics. They are the equilibrium play for the wealthier combatant in a war of attrition.

Front two: the $2.1 billion Medicare overbilling case. In 2011, whistleblower Benjamin Poehling, a former UnitedHealth employee, filed a False Claims Act suit alleging the company inflated patient diagnosis codes to extract higher Medicare Advantage payments. The DOJ took over the case in 2017. In May 2026, after fifteen years of litigation, Special Master Suzanne Segal recommended dismissal, finding that the government was "lacking any evidence" to prove UnitedHealth knowingly failed to return overpayments. "A mere possibility of an overpayment is not enough for the government to carry its burden," Segal wrote. UnitedHealth's shares rose 2.6% on the ruling. The company praised the finding: "The Special Master concluded there was no evidence to support the DOJ's claims we were overpaid or that we did anything wrong." Fifteen years. The government's own expert estimated overpayments exceeding $2 billion, which could have been tripled under the False Claims Act. The outcome: a recommendation for full dismissal. The insurer did not prove innocence. It outlasted the prosecution's ability to prove guilt.

Front three: the criminal and civil probes. In parallel with the Poehling case collapse, the Wall Street Journal reported that the DOJ's criminal healthcare-fraud unit had been investigating UNH's Medicare billing practices since at least mid-2025. UNH initially responded that it was "not aware of the 'launch' of any 'new' activity" and called fraud allegations "outrageous and false." It subsequently confirmed in an SEC filing that it was responding to both criminal and civil DOJ requests about its Medicare practices, that it had contacted the DOJ after media reports surfaced, and that it had "full confidence" in its practices. Shares fell 3.3%. The DOJ also maintains a separate antitrust investigation of UNH's acquisitions, started under Biden. The three probes run in parallel: criminal billing, civil billing, antitrust. None has produced a final judgment. The investigations function as a portfolio of threats, each individually insufficient to alter UNH's behavior, collectively creating an atmosphere of scrutiny without accountability. In any given quarter, UNH can truthfully tell investors that no probe has resulted in adverse findings.

The pattern across all three fronts is the same: delay converts collective pressure into individual attrition. The arbitration ruling splits the class. The special master ruling rewards a fifteen-year defense. The parallel probes generate headlines without penalties. Each of these is rational behavior by the insurer in a repeated game: the expected cost of fighting (legal fees × years) is less than the expected cost of settling (settlement amount + precedent that invites future claims + stock price signal that admits guilt). For UNH, with outside legal spend amounting to a rounding error on quarterly operating earnings of $8 billion, the math is unambiguous. Fight everything, settle nothing, and the system's own procedural complexity becomes the moat. The court's hands are tied. So are the plaintiffs'.

The Convergence Trap: Why Denial Rates Drop to the Mean, Not to Zero

In June 2026, MoneyGeek published its annual analysis of CMS Transparency in Coverage data for plan year 2024, covering 46 million in-network claims across all ACA Marketplace insurers. The headline result: UnitedHealthcare's ACA denial rate dropped from 34.2% to 19.1%, a 15-point decline and the largest single-insurer improvement in the dataset. Blue Cross Blue Shield of Alabama fell from 34.8% to 19%. Blue Cross Blue Shield of Montana dropped 27 points, from 39.4% to 12.5%.

Read that number again: 19.1%. The national average for 2024 was also 19.1%. UnitedHealthcare, the insurer that provoked a CEO assassination with what plaintiffs called a 90% algorithmic error rate, reformed its denial practices just enough to reach the exact industry average. Not below it. Not demonstrating genuine reform. To the mean. The company that was worst-in-class is now precisely average-in-class.

In competitive strategy, this has a name. Harold Hotelling demonstrated in 1929 that firms in a competitive market will converge toward the center of the distribution rather than differentiating at the extremes. The rational strategy for the outlier is not to become the best but to become unremarkable. By converging to 19.1%, UNH exits the spotlight. No regulator will sanction the company whose denial rate equals the industry average. No journalist will write about it. The 15-point drop generates favorable headlines ("UnitedHealthcare slashes denials!") while the destination (still denying one in five claims) avoids scrutiny because everyone else does too. This is reform as camouflage.

Hotelling's Law of Minimum Differentiation (1929)

In a spatial competition model, rational firms converge to the median position rather than differentiating at the extremes. Applied to regulatory compliance: when enforcement targets outliers, the optimal strategy is to become average, not exemplary. UNH's convergence from 34.2% to exactly 19.1% (the national mean) is a textbook case. The 15-point improvement generates reform optics; the 19.1% endpoint avoids regulatory salience. The insurer that was most visible became invisible by reaching the middle of the pack.

The composition of the remaining denials reveals why convergence to the mean is not the same as reform. MoneyGeek's breakdown of the 8.8 million denied claims in 2024 shows that 77% were denied for administrative reasons: missing information, duplicate submissions, late filings, or plan design exclusions. Medical necessity was the stated basis for fewer than one in four denials. Prior authorization accounted for just 12%. The structural implication: most of the 15-point improvement likely came from reducing paperwork denials and administrative friction, the easiest category to fix. The medical necessity denials, the ones that kill people, represent only 9% of the total. An insurer can cut its headline denial rate in half without touching a single clinical coverage decision.

The appeal data confirms the pattern. Of 8.8 million denied claims in 2024, 0.2% were appealed internally. When patients did appeal, insurers upheld the denial 56% of the time. Fewer than one per ten million denied claims reached external review with an independent reviewer. Forty percent of Marketplace enrollees did not know they had a right to external appeal. The denominator is the story. At 0.2% appeal rates, denial remains effectively unreviewable for the 99.8% who never challenge it. A company that reduces its denial rate from 34% to 19% has improved 1.28 million patients' immediate outcomes (15% of 8.5M claims at its volume of 6.4M), but the structural incentive to deny aggressively remains intact as long as patients rarely appeal and rarely win when they do.

Meanwhile, the market rewarded the convergence. On August 14, the day Mangione pled guilty and told a federal judge "I shot Mr. Thompson in Manhattan," UNH shares rose 0.55% to close at $401.53. By August 18, the stock had declined to $393.93, continuing a drift that began after the plea-day pop. The market priced in the criminal resolution in a single session, then returned to fundamental concerns. The stock has recovered from its mid-2025 trough near $260 to roughly $394 as of August 18, still 37% below its all-time high of $630 set three weeks before the shooting. But the trajectory is clear. Q2 2026 earnings showed a medical cost ratio improving to 86.7% (from 89.4% a year earlier), operating earnings jumping 55%, and management raising full-year guidance. The company has demonstrated to Wall Street that it can be profitable at 19.1% denial rates. The denial rate reform is not squeezing margins. It is pricing in the new equilibrium.

On August 17, 2026, three days after the guilty plea, Mark Cuban posted that 61% of Americans are "part of the healthcare problem" because they own health insurance stocks through retirement accounts and index funds. UNH is a component of the S&P 500, the Dow Jones Industrial Average, and virtually every target-date fund in the country. A teacher with a 401(k) invested in a Vanguard Target Retirement 2040 fund is, through the index, a UNH shareholder. As a patient, she wants lower denial rates. As a shareholder, she benefits from the operating leverage that denials provide. This dual identity creates what institutional economists call the "universal owner problem": when the same individuals are simultaneously harmed by and invested in the entity causing the harm, the feedback loop that should discipline the firm is short-circuited. The teacher-shareholder's retirement portfolio gained value from UNH's recovery to $395. Her insurance claim was denied by the same company last month. Both events are individually rational. Together they describe a system where the victims fund their own victimization.

The convergence trap predicts what comes next. If UNH at 19.1% no longer attracts disproportionate regulatory attention, the pressure that drove the 15-point improvement dissipates. Other insurers that worsened (Oscar Health rose to 25.3%, Molina to 22%) become the new outliers but lack UNH's political salience. The reform energy that moved UNH does not transfer to smaller carriers because the public narrative was always about UnitedHealthcare specifically, not about the industry's structural incentive to deny. The 19.1% average becomes the new floor rather than a waypoint toward genuine reform. And one in five claims continues to be denied, with 99.8% of those denials going unchallenged, at the industry average that nobody is trying to change.

The Security Premium: Cross-Industry Contagion

The Thompson shooting did not just reshape healthcare policy. It created a permanent cost increase across corporate America that has no precedent in the modern history of executive compensation.

Equilar's analysis of the Equilar 500, published in collaboration with Harvard Law School's Forum on Corporate Governance, found that 31.3% of S&P 500 companies provided security perquisites to executives in 2024, a 47.6% increase from 2021. Average spending on security perks more than doubled, from $43,068 in 2021 to $94,276 in 2024. These are aggregate figures across all industries. The Thompson shooting occurred in December 2024. The proxy disclosures, which reflect full-year 2024 spending, capture the immediate recalibration.

The healthcare sector's response was the sharpest. CVS Health reported $293,151 in executive security spending for 2024, up from $44,148 in 2023, a 564% increase. Elevance Health spent $129,600, up from $1,485, an 8,627% increase. Johnson & Johnson quadrupled its security spending to approximately $103,000 for CEO Joaquin Duato and disclosed for the first time that executives now travel with an armed driver in a secure company vehicle for "all travel" after an internal security assessment citing "increased threats." Eli Lilly spent $73,630 on CEO David Ricks' security, its first-ever disclosure of such costs. UNH itself reported $1.7 million in executive security spending in 2024.

But the contagion extended far beyond healthcare. Broadcom, a semiconductor company, spent $1.37 million protecting CEO Hock Tan. Lockheed Martin, a defense contractor, increased security spending from $149,069 to $1.34 million, citing "explicit threats received by leadership." These are companies with no connection to health insurance, no exposure to claim denial backlash, no reason to expect the specific grievances that motivated Mangione. The risk recalculation was not industry-specific. It was existential: any CEO whose company generates public resentment could become a target. AWS senior security consultant Taimur Ijlal predicted spending would continue climbing, signaling "a broad reset in board thinking." Alert:24, WTW's special contingency risk unit, issued a formal alert identifying banking, oil and gas, tobacco, defense, technology, and real estate as sectors facing elevated risk.

In game-theoretic terms, the security premium is a negative externality that propagated through the corporate governance system like a contagion. One act of violence in one industry imposed a permanent cost on every publicly traded company in every industry. The aggregate annual security spending increase across the S&P 500 can be estimated: if 31.3% of 500 companies now spend an average of $94,276 (up from approximately $43,068 for 21.2% of companies in 2021), the incremental annual spend is roughly $8.3 million across the index. This is trivial in absolute terms. But the second-order costs are not: canceled public events (UNH moved its 2025 investor day virtual), biometric IDs replacing badges, armed escorts at shareholder meetings, executive home fortification, the permanent loss of the "accessible CEO" as a corporate governance archetype. Brian Thompson had no security detail when he was killed outside his own investor conference. No Fortune 500 CEO will make that choice again.

The game theory produces a paradox. The security spending is individually rational for each company but collectively irrational for the corporate sector. It is a pure deadweight loss: resources spent preventing violence rather than creating value. Worse, it signals to the public that executives believe they deserve protection from the people their companies serve. When J&J mandates armed drivers for all executive travel, the message to patients paying $12,000 per year for health insurance (50% more than any other developed nation, per Global Finance Magazine) is that the company has priced in the possibility that its customers might kill its leaders. That pricing is itself an admission about the relationship between the corporation and the public it ostensibly serves.

Game Theory Concept
Negative Externality Contagion: The Cross-Domain Tax on Corporate Governance

In standard externality theory (Pigou, 1920), the cost of a harmful activity is borne by parties who did not choose to participate. The Thompson shooting created a negative externality of unusual scope: a single act of violence in the healthcare sector imposed permanent security costs on companies in semiconductors, defense, pharmaceuticals, and every other industry where executives face public visibility. The mechanism is reputational contagion through threat-model updating. Before December 2024, corporate boards modeled executive assassination as a negligible risk. After December 2024, boards across all sectors updated their threat models to include "economically motivated violence by grievance-driven actors." This single Bayesian update, triggered by one data point, shifted the equilibrium security spending for the entire S&P 500. The Thompson shooting was not a healthcare event. It was a corporate governance event that happened to originate in healthcare. The security premium it created is permanent because the updated threat model cannot be un-learned.

The Proxy Revelation: When Annual Reports Become Threat Assessments

The 2024 proxy data in the Security Premium section captured the initial shock. The 2025 proxy season, analyzed by Equilar in collaboration with the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance in April 2026, reveals something more significant: the spending is not reverting to pre-shooting levels. It is accelerating. Over one-third of S&P 500 companies now provide security perquisites to executives, up 12.8% from 2024. The median value of those perquisites reached $130,468, a 20% increase from the prior year. Among the top decile of spenders, median security expenses exceed $1.1 million, reflecting what the Compensation Advisory Partners study describes as "comprehensive protection models rather than discretionary perquisites." The trend is not stabilizing. It is compounding.

Health insurers, the industry at the epicenter, have filed the most revealing disclosures. UnitedHealth Group reported $1.66 million in personal and home security spending for five named executives in its latest proxy statement, noting the services are necessary "given the risks associated with executive officer positions." Oscar Health, led by former Aetna CEO Mark Bertolini, disclosed $361,094 in personal security for Bertolini alone, including $226,691 for dedicated security personnel and $75,000 for a third-party security assessment. Oscar's proxy was explicit about the cause: "We believe that the amounts paid by the Company for security services have been reasonable and necessary in light of the current climate and security risk profile for executives in the health insurance industry." CVS, Elevance, and Synchrony each filed new risk factor disclosures citing "protest activity" and "the UnitedHealthcare shooting" alongside updated executive protection programs. These filings are not corporate boilerplate. They are public admissions, under penalty of securities fraud, that the shooting permanently altered the risk calculus for anyone who runs a health insurance company.

The regulatory apparatus is now adapting to the new reality. The SEC Staff has begun examining whether executive security costs are properly classified as "perquisites" (compensation requiring detailed disclosure) or as expenses "directly and integrally related to the executive's duties" (which could be disclosed differently or exempted). At the SEC's June 2025 Roundtable on Executive Compensation Disclosure, participants debated whether mandating private aircraft for security reasons, a cost that can exceed $500,000 per executive annually, should be counted as a perk at all. BlackRock, the world's largest asset manager with over $11 trillion under management, updated its voting guidelines in 2026 to explicitly reference executive security perquisites, stating that it seeks to "understand the rationale for certain benefits, such as security, and whether the compensation committee regularly evaluates their appropriateness." When BlackRock asks a question in its voting guidelines, compensation committees across the S&P 500 recalibrate their disclosures. The Chertoff Group, founded by former DHS Secretary Michael Chertoff, reported that its corporate security consulting business more than doubled since the Thompson killing, the attempted assassination of Donald Trump in Butler, Pennsylvania, and the 2023 Hamas attack on Israel.

The game-theoretic significance is not the spending itself but what the disclosure mechanism reveals. SEC proxy rules force every S&P 500 company to disclose security spending above $10,000 once per year, in the same filing season, to the same audience of institutional investors. This creates a simultaneous information revelation event: every board's private assessment of executive threat level becomes public on the same timeline. The 2025 proxy season is the first full-year measurement of post-shooting equilibrium. Its verdict is unambiguous. When a third of the S&P 500 increases security spending by 20% in a single year, the filings are not individual corporate decisions. They are a collective, involuntary referendum on whether one act of violence permanently altered the corporate governance landscape. Each company's disclosure validates the threat assessment embedded in every other company's disclosure. No board can unilaterally reduce security spending when its peers are increasing it, because the marginal cost of a security failure now includes not just the physical risk but the fiduciary liability of having spent less than the disclosed industry norm. The result is a compliance ratchet: spending increases because disclosure increases because spending increases.

And each filing that names "the UnitedHealthcare incident" as justification performs one additional function. It converts the company's own SEC disclosure into evidence for the proposition that the shooting worked. When Oscar Health tells the SEC that its CEO requires $361,094 in protection because of "the current climate and security risk profile for executives in the health insurance industry," it is filing a document, under penalty of securities fraud, that confirms the threat Mangione created is real, persistent, and expensive enough to merit shareholder notice. UNH's own proxy is the most revealing of all: by disclosing $1.66 million in executive protection, the company whose CEO was killed is telling its shareholders that the risk of another such attack is sufficiently high to justify permanent, material expenditure. The victim's own annual report validates the deterrence thesis.

Game Theory Concept
Mandatory Disclosure as Involuntary Coordination (Banerjee, 1992; Grossman & Hart, 1980)

SEC proxy disclosure rules create a mechanism that Banerjee's (1992) herding model and Grossman and Hart's (1980) mandatory disclosure theory illuminate from different angles. Banerjee showed that when agents observe others' actions and update sequentially, rational herding can produce behavior that reflects social information rather than private signals. In the proxy context, each company's security spending disclosure becomes a public signal that subsequent filers incorporate into their own decisions. A board reviewing its 2025 security budget can observe that 34% of S&P 500 peers have already disclosed security perks. Not matching them creates fiduciary exposure: if a CEO is harmed after the board chose not to provide industry-standard protection, the proxy record documents the decision against the disclosed norm. Grossman and Hart showed that mandatory disclosure eliminates the option of strategic silence: companies cannot hide their assessment of executive risk because the SEC requires it. The combination produces involuntary coordination. Companies that would privately choose to spend less on security are forced to match the disclosed norm, because the disclosure itself creates the norm. The 12.8% year-over-year increase is not 500 independent board decisions. It is a coordination equilibrium enforced by the disclosure mechanism. One bullet created a new norm. SEC proxy rules enforce it. And no individual board can defect.

The Coverage Vacuum: ACA Collapse as Denominator Erosion

While the Medicaid contraction removes 5-10 million from coverage through legislative cuts, a parallel collapse is unfolding through market mechanisms. The enhanced premium tax credits that fueled record ACA Marketplace enrollment expired at the end of 2025. By February 2026, effectuated Marketplace enrollment had dropped from 22.1 million to 19.2 million, a 13% decline in a single year. KFF projects the average effectuated enrollment for 2026 will fall to approximately 17.5 million, representing a loss of 4.8 million enrollees from the Marketplace alone. A separate KFF analysis estimates 8.2 million additional people will become uninsured from ACA Marketplace changes, with more than half concentrated in three states: Florida (2.2 million), Texas (1.7 million), and Georgia (690,000). The coverage losses disproportionately hit Southern red states that saw the most rapid enrollment growth under the enhanced credits.

The mechanics of the collapse reveal who leaves first. Average monthly premium payments rose 58%, from $113 to $178, after the credits expired. Consumers above the subsidy cliff, just 7% of 2025 enrollment, accounted for 48% of the total decline. Young adults aged 18 to 34 comprised 46% of the drop, with sign-ups falling by 542,000. These are the healthiest, lowest-utilization members, the ones whose premiums cross-subsidize the sickest enrollees in the risk pool. A KFF survey found that 9% of 2025 Marketplace enrollees had already become uninsured by early 2026, and one in six returning enrollees reported they were not confident they could afford premiums for the full year. Plan cancellations were up 24% compared to the prior year.

The downstream effect hit hospitals immediately. HCA Healthcare, the nation's largest for-profit hospital system, reported a $150 million reduction in adjusted EBITDA from ACA coverage changes in Q1 2026. Marketplace-equivalent admissions fell 15% year-over-year. Of an estimated 16% increase in uninsured admissions, more than half stemmed from the coverage shift. Patients who maintained coverage shifted from silver to bronze plans, accepting higher out-of-pocket costs in exchange for lower premiums. The shift was not a choice about care quality. It was a financial survival strategy.

Game Theory Concept
Dual Denominator Erosion: Adverse Selection Meets Coverage Contraction

Akerlof's (1970) "lemons" model predicts that when information asymmetry drives the healthiest participants out of a market, the remaining pool becomes sicker, premiums rise, and more participants exit, creating a "death spiral." The ACA's individual mandate and enhanced credits prevented this spiral by keeping young, healthy enrollees in the pool. The credit expiration triggered the classic adverse selection exit: the youngest, healthiest members leave first (18-34 year-olds = 46% of the decline), the risk pool deteriorates, and premiums rise further. Combined with the OBBBA's Medicaid contraction, the result is dual denominator erosion: the population benefiting from post-shooting prior authorization reforms is shrinking from two directions simultaneously. The ACA collapse removes 4.8 million from the commercial insurance market. The Medicaid cuts remove 5-10 million from government coverage. The 6.5 million prior authorizations eliminated since June 2025 apply to a denominator that is 10-15 million smaller than when the reforms began. The game-theoretic paradox is acute: insurers reformed their most visible practices under public pressure, but the population exposed to those practices is contracting faster than the reform expands access within it. The equilibrium is not "better coverage for all." It is "better rules for fewer people."

The coverage vacuum creates a feedback loop that undermines the reform narrative. If 8.2 million people lose ACA Marketplace coverage and 5-10 million lose Medicaid, between 13 and 18 million Americans will face the healthcare system with no coverage at all. They will not encounter prior authorization because they will not encounter insurance. They will not be denied claims because they will not file claims. They will delay care until it becomes emergent, then present at emergency departments, generating uncompensated care costs that hospitals pass along to insurers and insured patients through cost-shifting. The prior authorization reforms that followed the Thompson shooting are genuine improvements for the shrinking number who remain covered. But the structural irony is that the political crisis that forced those reforms coincided with the policy decisions that removed millions from coverage entirely. The game's rules improved. Its roster shrank.

The 2027 rate filings confirm the spiral is accelerating. In July 2026, KFF's Peterson Health System Tracker analysis of preliminary filings from 77 ACA Marketplace insurers across 16 states and Washington, D.C., found a proposed median premium increase of 14% for 2027. This would be the second consecutive year of double-digit increases, following a finalized 20% median increase for 2026. If approved, typical Marketplace premiums will have risen by more than one-third between 2025 and 2027. Twenty of the 77 insurers are requesting increases above 20%. Cynthia Cox, KFF's director of the ACA program, called it a "triple whammy" for consumers: higher 2026 premiums, expired tax credits, and now another double-digit hike. Insurers cite a 10% increase in underlying medical care and prescription drug costs, driven partly by GLP-1 adoption, specialty oncology drugs, and labor shortages, exceeding the 8% average of recent years. The adverse selection mechanics are textbook: the healthiest leave first, the risk pool deteriorates, premiums rise, more leave. On July 16, 2026, a federal court in Maryland temporarily blocked eight provisions of the 2027 benefit rule (City of Columbus v. Kennedy), preserving standardized plan requirements and blocking expanded catastrophic eligibility. But even the court's intervention only slows the erosion; it cannot reverse the premium increases that drove the initial enrollment collapse.

The Provider Exodus: Administrative Burden as Workforce Attrition

The prior authorization system imposes costs that never appear in an insurer's financial statements. In April 2026, the Medical Group Management Association published its annual Regulatory Burden Report based on a survey of over 230 group practices. The headline: 95% of practices reported their regulatory and administrative burden had increased over the prior three years. Ninety percent said prior authorization requirements specifically had increased in the past year. Forty percent of practices now employ three or more full-time administrative staff per physician solely to manage prior authorization, denials, audits, and quality reporting. Three of the top five administrative burdens identified were exclusively tied to Medicare Advantage: prior authorization, claim denials, and automatic downcoding. Seventy-nine percent of practices experiencing a shift toward Medicare Advantage reported a negative operational impact. Anders Gilberg, MGMA's senior vice president of government affairs, quantified the systemic cost: upward of three full-time administrative employees per physician, while a full quarter of all U.S. healthcare spending goes to administrative overhead, the highest proportion of any developed nation.

The physician-level data is worse. The AMA's 2024 physician survey found that practices complete an average of 43 prior authorizations per physician per week, consuming 12 hours of physician and staff time. Ninety-five percent of physicians reported that the process contributes to their burnout. Eighty-seven percent said prior authorization leads to higher overall healthcare utilization, not lower. Sixty-nine percent reported ineffective initial treatments due to step-therapy requirements. Twenty-nine percent reported that prior authorization caused serious adverse events, including hospitalization, in patients under their care. The MGMA 2026 report confirmed the downstream consequence: 77% of respondents cited regulatory burden as a major contributor to physician burnout, and that burnout is prompting physicians to reduce work hours, leave practice, or retire early, resulting in longer wait times, fewer available providers, and reduced access to care.

The clinical evidence is accumulating in peer-reviewed journals. A 2026 JAMA Neurology scoping review by Gotlieb et al. examined the barriers and consequences of prior authorization across six neurologic conditions. Their findings were grim: 60% of studies documented care delays attributable to prior authorization, 25% documented increased disease activity, and roughly half of multiple sclerosis patients awaiting disease-modifying therapy approval experienced relapses during the delay period. Separately, a Consultant360 survey of 501 outpatient physicians found that 81% viewed prior authorization as a major or significant barrier to clinical care, 64% attributed utilization management as a contributing factor in their burnout, and 52% spent between 6 and 21 or more hours per week on utilization management paperwork alone.

Game Theory Concept
Exit, Voice, and Loyalty: Provider Attrition as Market Signal

Hirschman's (1970) framework distinguishes three responses to institutional decline: exit (leaving the institution), voice (attempting to reform it from within), and loyalty (staying despite dissatisfaction). The prior authorization system has engineered a provider response that follows Hirschman's prediction with precision. Voice has been loud: the AMA, MGMA, and specialty societies have lobbied for reform for decades, producing the legislative pipeline now moving through Congress. But voice alone has not changed the equilibrium because the cost of voice falls on the same actors bearing the cost of the problem. Each hour a physician spends advocating for reform is an hour not spent on patient care, and the 12 hours per week already consumed by PA paperwork leaves little margin. So physicians exercise exit. They reduce hours, close practices, retire early, or leave clinical medicine entirely. The game-theoretic significance is that exit functions as a market signal that voice cannot provide. When a rural practice closes because its PA administrative costs exceed its reimbursement, the resulting "provider desert" becomes a visible, measurable harm that legislators can quantify. Provider exit converts an invisible tax (administrative overhead absorbed within practice budgets) into a visible failure (patients unable to find a physician). The 77% of practices reporting burnout-driven workforce attrition means the prior authorization system is cannibalizing the very supply of providers whose services it claims to manage. This is not a side effect. It is a second-order mechanism design failure: the system designed to control costs is destroying the capacity to deliver care.

The feedback loop is self-reinforcing. As physicians exit, the remaining providers absorb higher patient volumes, face more prior authorization requests per physician, spend more time on administrative tasks, and burn out faster. The MGMA report documented practices warning that continued participation in Medicare Advantage "may become financially unsustainable without meaningful reform." This is the structural irony: the insurer's cost-control mechanism has become the leading driver of the provider shortage that will increase costs. Administrative overhead consumes 25 cents of every healthcare dollar spent in America. The prior authorization reforms triggered by the Thompson shooting reduced the number of authorization requests, but they did not touch the per-request burden, the portal fragmentation across dozens of insurer-specific systems, or the fundamental mismatch between clinical time and bureaucratic time. The providers who remain in practice are the loyal ones in Hirschman's framework. Their loyalty is finite.

The Parity Paradox: Mental Health as Prior Authorization's Shadow System

While the Thompson shooting created a political ratchet around medical and surgical prior authorization, a parallel system operates with almost no public scrutiny. In May 2025, the Government Accountability Office published GAO-25-107342, the first congressional report dedicated to prior authorization of behavioral health services in Medicare Advantage. The findings were stark. Eight of nine MA organizations reviewed (collectively covering 45% of all MA beneficiaries) required prior authorization for inpatient behavioral health services. Seven of nine used internal coverage criteria, not Medicare criteria, to make those authorization decisions. For partial hospitalization programs, six of nine required PA and three used internal criteria. For transcranial magnetic stimulation, used to treat severe depression, seven of nine required PA. Traditional fee-for-service Medicare requires prior authorization for zero behavioral health services. The behavioral health PA system in Medicare Advantage is entirely an invention of private insurers, applied to a population that federal law says should receive at least equivalent coverage.

The GAO asked CMS why it had not targeted behavioral health services in its annual program audit reviews of MA prior authorization denials. CMS's answer was revealing: behavioral health services "make up a small percentage of MA services," and the agency "could not commit to targeting them at this time." The GAO recommended targeting behavioral health in both existing denial audits and the new internal coverage criteria reviews planned for 2026. CMS took the recommendation "under advisement." As of May 2025, CMS had not released final plans for the criteria reviews or indicated whether they would include any behavioral health services. The regulator responsible for ensuring MA beneficiaries receive equitable coverage has never specifically examined whether they do for the conditions most likely to involve crisis-level stakes in treatment timing.

This oversight vacuum exists against a backdrop of documented harm. Provider associations told the GAO that prior authorization delays for substance use disorder treatment, even by an hour, can lead to negative patient outcomes because the window of readiness for treatment is fragile and time-limited. Behavioral health denial rates run two to three times higher than the medical/surgical average across the industry. Authorization renewal denials for behavioral health run 15 to 25 percent without updated documentation. The Wit v. United Behavioral Health case, litigated for over a decade in the Northern District of California, found that UBH had rubber-stamped claims for short-term behavioral health treatment and summarily denied claims for long-term treatment, affecting over 67,000 denied claims. A separate DOL and New York AG investigation resulted in a $16 million settlement after determining that UBH subjected all outpatient psychotherapy to utilization review while only a "handful" of medical and surgical services faced the same scrutiny.

The enforcement mechanism meant to prevent this disparity is being dismantled. On March 30, 2026, the Departments of Labor, HHS, and Treasury informed a federal court that they would not defend the Biden administration's 2024 Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act final rule, the first major update to the 2008 MHPAEA in a decade. The rule had required health plans to analyze and document their non-quantitative treatment limitations (including prior authorization) and ensure they are no more restrictive for behavioral health than for medical and surgical care. The ERISA Industry Committee, representing large employers, had challenged the rule as exceeding statutory authority and creating an "impossible standard." The Trump administration agreed, pausing enforcement in May 2025 and issuing a formal non-enforcement policy covering the entire litigation period plus an additional 18 months. The agencies said they anticipate "significant revisions." The practical effect is that the only federal mechanism specifically designed to prevent discriminatory PA practices in behavioral health has been suspended indefinitely.

ProPublica's investigation revealed the enforcement infrastructure that existed even before the suspension. The Employee Benefits Security Administration, which safeguards workplace benefits for 150 million Americans, has one investigator for every 7,700 health plans. Between February 2021 and July 2024, during an administration that supported its mission, EBSA conducted 150 investigations and issued just 70 letters finding parity violations. The enforcement gap between medical/surgical PA reform (backed by six federal bills, bipartisan Senate investigations, and public outrage) and behavioral health PA enforcement (understaffed, underfunded, and now formally suspended) creates a strategic opportunity for rational insurers. The same industry that is conspicuously eliminating medical and surgical prior authorizations under political pressure faces no equivalent pressure on behavioral health. In some states, the pressure runs in the opposite direction: Ohio announced in mid-2026 that it would require prior authorization for virtually all levels of behavioral health care under Medicaid after patients reach certain treatment limits. Kentucky reinstated full PA and utilization management requirements for Medicaid behavioral health services in June 2025, explicitly more restrictive than pre-pandemic policy.

Game Theory Concept
Strategic Substitution: Displacement to the Least-Regulated Channel

When regulatory pressure closes one avenue of rent extraction, the rational profit-maximizing actor does not internalize the loss. It shifts activity to the least-regulated alternative that preserves the same revenue function. This is the behavioral analog of regulatory arbitrage in financial markets and of Hotelling's spatial model (1929) applied to regulatory geography rather than physical geography: firms migrate toward the competitive position where regulatory oversight is minimized. In the PA context, the Thompson shooting made medical and surgical prior authorization the most politically costly form of denial. Behavioral health PA is structurally identical in mechanism (require pre-approval, apply internal criteria, deny at rates exceeding Medicare), but occupies a separate regulatory domain with weaker enforcement, less public visibility, and an oversight agency (DOL/EBSA) whose enforcement capacity is one-seventh that of CMS. The rational insurer response is to reduce friction in the high-visibility channel (medical/surgical PA) while maintaining or expanding friction in the low-visibility channel (behavioral health PA). The GAO's finding that CMS has never audited behavioral health PA denials, combined with the suspension of MHPAEA enforcement, means this substitution can occur with effectively zero probability of detection. The displacement is self-concealing: the same insurer can truthfully claim it eliminated 30% of prior authorizations while shifting denial pressure to a patient population whose treatment-abandonment risk is higher, whose appeal capacity is lower, and whose conditions carry a stigma that reduces public sympathy. The parity law was designed to prevent this exact dynamic. Its enforcement has been suspended.

The structural irony is complete. The Thompson shooting catalyzed the most significant PA reform in decades for cancer treatment, cardiac care, and surgical procedures. For the one-fifth of Medicare beneficiaries with behavioral health conditions, the reform ratchet does not reach. They exist in a system where private insurers impose authorization requirements that traditional Medicare does not require, use proprietary internal criteria that CMS has never audited, face denial rates two to three times the medical average, and rely on a parity enforcement mechanism that was underfunded before its enforcement was suspended and whose revival depends on a rulemaking process explicitly designed by its opponents. The game theory predicts the outcome: when the political cost of denial concentrates on one patient population, the rational insurer displaces denial pressure onto the population with the least political voice. That population is disproportionately people with substance use disorders, severe mental illness, and suicidal ideation, patients for whom a one-hour delay in treatment authorization is not an inconvenience but a clinical crisis.

The Settlement Calculus: Why Insurers Profit Even When They Lose

The Attrition Shield explains how insurers outlast litigation. A companion question is more disturbing: what happens when insurers actually lose? The proton beam radiation therapy settlement wave of 2026 provides the clearest quantitative answer. In May 2026, Anthem (now Elevance Health) agreed to pay $3.625 million to settle a class action brought by approximately 140 prostate cancer patients whose requests for proton beam radiation therapy had been denied. The insurer had classified PBRT as "experimental, investigational, or unproven" and refused coverage under employer-sponsored ERISA plans. In June 2026, Aetna settled a parallel class action for $3.4 million, covering 71 cancer patients denied the same treatment between January 2015 and March 2024, with individual payouts ranging from $12,000 to $48,000 per patient. Final approval for the Anthem settlement is scheduled for August 19, 2026, before Judge William L. Campbell Jr. in the Middle District of Tennessee.

The arithmetic is what matters. A course of proton beam radiation therapy for prostate cancer costs between $30,000 and $95,000, depending on the facility and treatment plan. If Anthem denied 140 patients to avoid paying an average of $60,000 each, the total avoided claims were approximately $8.4 million. The settlement cost $3.625 million, roughly 43 cents on the dollar. But the 140 patients in the settlement class are only those identified in discovery who actually sought coverage, were denied, and remained in the litigation. They represent the tail end of a distribution. The vast majority of patients denied PBRT coverage never filed suit. Some paid out of pocket. Some switched to conventional radiation, which is less targeted and carries higher side-effect profiles for surrounding tissue. Some delayed treatment. The insurer's true savings include every denied patient who absorbed the cost or changed treatment plans without litigating. If even 500 patients were denied PBRT coverage across the policy period and only 140 litigated, Anthem avoided $30 million in claims and paid $3.625 million, a net gain of over $26 million from the denial policy even after losing in court.

ProPublica's investigation of the PBRT denial apparatus revealed the mechanism in granular detail. When a Louisiana physician named Jeffrey Fuller sought coverage for his patient's proton therapy, the insurer routed the case through five different reviewers at four different companies in 19 days. Each reviewer denied the claim. Each cited the same internal guidelines from AIM Specialty Health (now Carelon Medical Benefits Management), Anthem's own subsidiary. The "independent" medical review board selected by Louisiana's Department of Insurance quoted the same AIM guidelines. Five reviewers, four companies, 19 days, one set of proprietary criteria, one outcome. The appeal process was designed to produce the appearance of independent review while generating the same answer at every node. This is not a failure of the mechanism. It is the mechanism working exactly as designed.

The settlement terms confirm the asymmetry. Anthem class members must submit proof of qualifying out-of-pocket payments, including provider name, address, date of service, description of services, and charges. Cancer patients who were denied coverage, found alternative funding, received treatment, and survived must now produce itemized receipts to recover a fraction of what they paid. The claim deadline is October 10, 2026. Patients who died during or after treatment are represented by their estates, which must produce the same documentation. The administrative burden of proving you were wrongly denied coverage for cancer treatment falls on the patient, not the insurer that denied it.

Game Theory Concept
The Denial Lottery: Expected Value Calculation Under Asymmetric Litigation Capacity

The insurer's decision to deny a claim can be modeled as a lottery with known parameters. Let C be the cost of the claim if approved, P(sue) be the probability the denied patient files suit, and S be the expected settlement cost if the suit succeeds. The expected cost of denial is: E(deny) = P(sue) x S. The expected cost of approval is: E(approve) = C. Denial is the dominant strategy whenever P(sue) x S < C. In the PBRT case: C = ~$60,000 per patient. The KFF finding that only 0.2% of denied patients appeal externally, combined with the further attrition from appeal to lawsuit, suggests P(sue) < 0.05. Even if S = C (full reimbursement), the expected cost of denial is 0.05 x $60,000 = $3,000, versus $60,000 to approve. The insurer saves $57,000 per denial in expected value. The settlement data confirms this: $3.625M / 140 patients = ~$25,900 per litigating patient, well below the $60,000 average claim cost. The Anthem settlement thus reveals that denial remained the profit-maximizing strategy even for the patients who fought back, because the settlement amount was less than the avoided claim cost. For the hundreds of patients who never sued, the denial generated pure profit. This is why denial rates do not converge to zero even under political pressure: the expected-value calculation makes denial a dominant strategy for any individual claim, regardless of the reputational or regulatory cost at the portfolio level. The equilibrium is not "deny everything." It is "deny at the rate where the marginal political cost of the next denial equals the marginal expected savings," which is precisely the convergence to the industry average that the CMS data reveals.

The PBRT settlement wave is not an isolated case. It is the quantitative proof of a structural feature. Anthem's $13 million mental health parity settlement (Collins v. Anthem, E.D.N.Y.) covered behavioral health denials affecting thousands of members, yielding nominal payments of $100 per person. The Aetna-Optum administrative fee settlement ($8.35 million, preliminarily approved 2026) covered disguised charges across an entire patient population. In each case, the settlement arithmetic favors the insurer: the cost of settling is less than the cost of the denied claims, and both are less than the cost of approving every claim in the first place. The litigation system, designed to impose accountability, instead validates the denial strategy by pricing it below the cost of compliance. The patients who receive $25,000 from the Anthem settlement for a $60,000 cancer treatment they paid for out of pocket did not receive justice. They received a discount on the injustice, calculated to be cheaper for the insurer than the alternative of never denying them in the first place.

The Reprocess Ratchet: Litigation as the Only Effective Coverage Expansion Mechanism

The Settlement Calculus establishes that insurers profit even when they lose in court. The corollary is more consequential: litigation has become the de facto mechanism through which coverage classifications change. Not clinical evidence. Not regulatory action. Not patient advocacy. Lawsuits. Across at least four distinct treatment categories in 2026 alone, the pattern is identical. An insurer classifies a treatment as "experimental, investigational, or unproven." Patients are denied coverage for years or decades. A class action forces discovery. The insurer agrees, under litigation pressure, to reprocess denied claims and reverse its coverage position. The classification that blocked thousands of patients is quietly reclassified as medically necessary, not because the clinical evidence changed but because the litigation cost finally exceeded the denial savings.

The prosthetics case is the most structurally revealing. In Trujillo v. UnitedHealth Group (C.D. Cal.), a class of approximately 1,000 patients had been denied coverage for prosthetic devices because UnitedHealth determined they sought devices "more advanced than the minimum available." The original complaint was filed in December 2017. Nine years of litigation produced a settlement in which UNH agreed to reprocess denied claims, reimburse up to $22,500 per person, and change future procedures to provide more specific explanations when denying prosthetic coverage and to identify alternative devices. Preliminary approval came on July 19, 2026. The plaintiffs' attorneys requested $1.5 million in fees, due August 22. The arithmetic of the denial is embedded in the settlement terms: UNH had been denying patients functional prosthetic limbs, the devices that allow amputees to walk, grip, and perform daily activities, on the grounds that a cheaper, less functional device technically existed. The "minimum available" standard meant that a veteran who lost a leg could be denied a microprocessor knee (which adjusts to walking speed, prevents falls on stairs, and enables near-normal gait) because a basic mechanical hinge existed. For nine years, that was the coverage position. The settlement changed it.

The spinal surgery reversal is more explicit. Two class actions settled in April 2026 challenged UnitedHealth's classification of lumbar artificial disc replacement as "experimental." In March 2026, during the litigation, UNH changed its coverage position entirely, reclassifying lumbar ADR from experimental to "proven treatment that's medically necessary if certain criteria are met." A healthcare attorney involved in the settlement observed that it is "unusual to get a health plan" to change its coverage classification. The rarity is the point. The only mechanism that produced the change was litigation. The clinical evidence supporting lumbar ADR had not materially changed between 2017 (when patients first started being denied) and 2026 (when UNH reversed its position). What changed was the expected cost of continuing to defend the classification in federal court.

The lipedema litigation introduced a new variable: a federal judge who refused to let the settlement calculus operate on its terms. In Caldwell v. UnitedHealthcare (N.D. Cal.), Judge William Alsup reviewed a proposed class settlement for patients denied coverage for lipedema treatment, including liposuction, and rejected it. He found the initial settlement terms too favorable to the insurer and too restrictive of patient rights, characterizing the deal as one that enriched lawyers while limiting the class. UNH was forced to restructure the agreement: the revised terms provided unlimited coverage for all class members regardless of plan status, with no monetary cap on reimbursement. The judge denied UNH's motion to resolve the matter without trial. In a separate lipedema case in the Eastern District of North Carolina, Judge Boyle ordered UNH to pay $90,000 for six liposuction procedures after finding that UNH "denied the patient's coverage request using a set of records it knew was incomplete." The court determined that UNH had omitted material documentation from its review, including letters from medical providers, the patient's treatment history, and symptom documentation. The denial was not an error of judgment. It was an error of omission that the court found was deliberate.

The behavioral health settlement completes the pattern. In a Northern District of New York class action, Judge D'Agostino approved a $1.4 million settlement for an ERISA class of patients whose residential substance use disorder and mental health treatment had been denied as "experimental, investigational or unproven." Individual payouts ranged from $936 to $18,734. The same label that blocked cancer patients from proton therapy and amputees from functional prosthetics blocked people with substance use disorders from residential treatment. The "experimental" classification functions as a universal denial tool, applied across treatment categories with no clinical common denominator, retracted only when the cost of litigation exceeds the savings from denial. A minor but illustrative companion case: UNH settled a $2.5 million TCPA class action (W.D. Wash.) involving more than 800 members who received pre-recorded robocalls despite warnings to stop, yielding $98 to $1,425 per person. The pattern of institutional disregard for individual patient boundaries is not confined to clinical decisions.

Game Theory Concept
Revealed Preference via Litigation Settlement (Samuelson, 1938)

Paul Samuelson's (1938) theory of revealed preference holds that an agent's true preferences are disclosed by observed choices under constraint, not by stated policy. When an insurer agrees under litigation to reprocess claims and reverse a coverage classification from "experimental" to "medically necessary," the settlement constitutes a revealed preference that exposes the original classification as strategic rather than clinical. The pattern across four treatment categories in 2026 (prosthetics, spinal surgery, lipedema, residential behavioral health) reveals that "experimental/unproven" functions as a default denial label applied until litigation cost exceeds denial savings. This is the inverse of the Settlement Calculus: while the denial lottery shows that insurers profit from denying claims even when they lose, the reprocess ratchet shows that insurers' settlement behavior reveals their true assessment of medical necessity more accurately than their coverage policy does. The coverage policy says "experimental." The settlement says "we will reprocess every denied claim and change our future procedures." The revealed preference is dispositive. Judge Alsup's rejection of the first lipedema settlement introduces a new strategic player: the judiciary as settlement-floor enforcer. In standard class action settlements, the court rubber-stamps deals negotiated between sophisticated parties. Alsup's intervention, demanding unlimited coverage with no monetary cap, pushes the Denial Lottery equilibrium toward less profitable settlements by raising the floor on what counts as adequate relief. If other judges follow Alsup's lead, the expected settlement cost S in the Denial Lottery formula increases, narrowing the gap between E(deny) and E(approve). The reprocess ratchet would then accelerate: more categories reclassified, faster, because the expected savings from maintaining the "experimental" label shrink with each judicial intervention that demands real reprocessing rather than token payouts.

The reprocess ratchet exposes the central paradox of healthcare coverage in the United States. The insurer's official coverage policy is not a clinical document. It is a legal position, maintained until litigation makes it more expensive than compliance. The patients denied functional prosthetics for nine years, the spinal surgery patients classified as experimental until a federal lawsuit forced a reversal, the lipedema patients whose medical records were deliberately incomplete during review, the people with substance use disorders whose residential treatment was labeled unproven: none of them were denied because the clinical evidence was insufficient. They were denied because no one had yet sued. The only mechanism that reliably converts "experimental" to "medically necessary" is a class action complaint with discovery powers. That is not a healthcare system. It is a legal system with medical consequences.

The Reputation Game: Gold Cards and the Limits of Decentralized Reform

If prior authorization is a mechanism design failure, what does a well-designed mechanism look like? The most ambitious reform attempt is the gold card: a reputation-based exemption system that turns the repeated game between providers and insurers into a track record. Texas pioneered it in 2021 with House Bill 3459. The concept is elegant. If a physician's prior authorization requests for a specific service are approved at least 90% of the time over a 12-month evaluation period (originally six months, expanded by HB 3812 in 2025), the insurer must exempt that physician from PA requirements for that service. The exemption lasts until the next annual review. The physician earns a "gold card" that lets patients receive care without the administrative checkpoint.

In game-theoretic terms, the gold card converts a one-shot game (each PA request evaluated independently) into a repeated game with a reputation mechanism. Kreps and Wilson's (1982) sequential equilibrium and Milgrom and Roberts' (1982) work on reputation in repeated games formalized this insight: when a player's future payoffs depend on their current-period behavior, cooperation becomes self-enforcing. A physician who maintains a 90% approval rate is rewarded with reduced administrative burden. The insurer retains the ability to revoke the exemption if the rate falls below threshold in a subsequent period. Both players have incentives to sustain cooperation rather than defect.

The problem is implementation. Medscape's 2025 analysis of gold card programs across multiple states called results "underwhelming." In Texas, the law applies only to state-regulated commercial insurers and HMOs, which the Texas Medical Association estimates represent roughly one-fifth of the state's health insurance market. Employer-sponsored plans, which cover the majority of working Texans, are governed by ERISA and preempted from state insurance regulation. Medicare Advantage, where denial rates are highest and the Thompson shooting's political pressure is most concentrated, is federally regulated and untouched by state gold card laws. The gold card fixes the mechanism in the jurisdiction with the least power to enforce it.

Insurers have also found ways to narrow the program's reach. The minimum volume threshold (at least five PA requests per service in the evaluation period) excludes specialists who see fewer state-regulated patients, and the per-service granularity means a cardiologist who earns a gold card for echocardiograms still needs PA for stress tests. UnitedHealthcare's own implementation FAQ specifies that kidney, transplant, bariatric, and ventricular device services remain subject to "advance notification" regardless of gold card status. The exemption, in practice, covers the least controversial procedures for the most compliant providers in the smallest market segment.

Despite these limitations, the idea has propagated. By mid-2026, eleven states had adopted gold card variants: Arkansas, Colorado, Illinois, Louisiana, Michigan, New Mexico, Texas, Vermont, West Virginia, Wyoming, and Rhode Island (which eliminated PA entirely for routine primary care in a three-year pilot starting October 2025). Health Affairs' analysis of state legislative action counted 18 states passing PA reform legislation in 2025 alone, with at least 90 bills introduced across 30 states. The reforms range from process fixes (Alaska and Delaware mandating 24-to-72-hour turnaround for urgent requests, Indiana requiring automatic approval if insurers miss the deadline) to substantive restrictions (Montana banning PA for all insulin, Maryland requiring human oversight of any AI used in coverage decisions).

At the federal level, Congress introduced the GOLD CARD Act (bipartisan, first filed 2022, reintroduced 2023), which would extend the 90% exemption model to Medicare Advantage. It has not passed. But a different legislative track produced something more consequential. On February 3, 2026, President Trump signed the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026, which includes the most significant pharmacy benefit manager reform in the program's history. The CAA mandates rebate pass-through (PBMs can no longer retain manufacturer rebates), enhanced disclosure requirements, flat-fee service arrangements, and enhanced federal oversight of PBM compensation structures. The law takes effect January 1, 2028 for Part D and Medicare Advantage prescription drug plans. A companion DOL proposal (published January 30, 2026) would require PBMs and affiliated brokers to disclose compensation arrangements to ERISA plan fiduciaries. Together, these address the regulatory gap that the CMS-0057-F interoperability rule left open: federal PA reform originally excluded prescription drugs entirely. The CAA and CMS-0062-P (the drug PA proposed rule) are closing that gap from two directions.

The game theory of decentralized reform reveals a structural constraint. The National Council for Prescription Drug Programs estimates that prior authorization consumes roughly $35 billion in annual healthcare administrative spending, with nursing time alone equivalent to 100,000 full-time roles devoted to managing PA workflows. This is the cost of the mechanism, not the care. Reforms that reduce this cost in one jurisdiction but leave it intact in others create a classic Tiebout (1956) sorting problem: providers migrate toward less burdensome regulatory environments, patients in unreformed jurisdictions face longer waits, and insurers exploit the patchwork by shifting PA volume to markets where gold cards, AI bans, and timeline mandates do not apply.

Game Theory Concept
The Regulatory Patchwork as Tiebout Sorting

Charles Tiebout's (1956) model of local public goods predicts that mobile agents sort across jurisdictions offering different regulatory bundles. Applied to PA reform: 14 states have enacted AI denial bans, 11 have gold card programs, 18 passed PA reform legislation in 2025 alone, but 36 states have no binding restrictions on AI in coverage decisions. A patient in Iowa (gold card + AI ban + timeline mandate, effective July 2026) and a patient in Florida (no gold card, no AI ban, no state PA reform) hold policies from the same insurer but face different denial probabilities for the same procedure. The reform is real, but its reach is jurisdictional, not universal. The gap between states with comprehensive reform and states without creates an arbitrage opportunity that national insurers, operating across all 50 states, are uniquely positioned to exploit. The mechanism design solution (gold cards) works locally, but the problem it addresses (algorithmic denial at scale) is national. This is the fundamental limitation of decentralized reform in a market where the dominant players operate nationally.

The gold card experiment suggests that mechanism design can fix individual interactions but cannot solve the structural problem. A physician who earns a gold card in Texas still operates in a system where 80% of the state's insured population is covered by plans exempt from the gold card law. A state that bans AI denials pushes algorithmic review to the behavioral health channel, where no state has yet legislated. A federal rule that mandates electronic PA for drugs arrives in 2027 or 2028, years after the reforms it complements. Each reform is a local optimization. The global equilibrium remains: the system generates $35 billion in administrative friction annually, and the entities best positioned to reduce that friction are the same entities that profit from it.

The Surveillance Dividend: When Deniers Become Data Brokers

The information asymmetry at the core of this analysis has a dimension that extends beyond claims processing. The same entities that decide whether to approve or deny care are simultaneously harvesting and monetizing the data patients generate while seeking that care. On August 7, 2026, U.S. District Judge John J. Tharp Jr. in the Northern District of Illinois ruled on a class action alleging that the Blue Cross Blue Shield Association had embedded tracking tools from TikTok, Meta, Google, and LinkedIn on fepblue.org, the website used by more than 5.5 million federal employees, retirees, and family members to research coverage options, search for physicians, and look up medical information. The plaintiffs alleged that BCBS permitted these third parties to intercept users' health-related website activity, including searches for specific conditions and treatments, without consent.

Judge Tharp dismissed claims under the federal Electronic Communications Privacy Act, the Illinois Computer Tampering Act, the Illinois Eavesdropping Statute, and common law, largely on the grounds that the plaintiffs did not plausibly allege exposure of individually identifiable health information or conduct meeting the "surreptitious manner" threshold. But one claim survived: the California Invasion of Privacy Act, under which BCBS could be liable through aiding and abetting. Plaintiffs have until September 4, 2026, to amend their complaint. The partial survival is notable because CIPA lacks the "surreptitious manner" requirement that blocked the Illinois claims, suggesting that the legal question is not whether insurers share patient data with third parties but under which statutory framework that sharing becomes actionable.

The BCBS case is not isolated. In June 2026, Allina Health System, a Minnesota-based health system operating hospitals and clinics, agreed to a $12.5 million settlement to resolve a class action alleging it used pixel tracking on its website to share sensitive patient health data with third parties. The settlement covers portal users, bill-pay users, scheduling users, and non-portal patients from September 2018 through May 2026, encompassing nearly eight years of data collection. Final approval is scheduled for September 24, 2026. And in July 2026, Aetna and OptumHealth Care Solutions (a UnitedHealth Group subsidiary) finalized an $8.35 million settlement for using "dummy" billing codes to charge patients administrative fees disguised as medical expenses, a practice that fabricated data about the nature of services rendered. Together, these cases reveal a pattern: the entities controlling the claims pipeline are also monetizing the informational exhaust that patients produce while navigating that pipeline.

The game theory is Stigler's (1961) "The Economics of Information" applied to a market where one player controls both the adjudication of claims and the commercial value of the data generated by those claims. In Stigler's framework, the cost of information search determines market power. A patient searching for coverage information on an insurer's website is engaged in precisely the kind of costly search Stigler described. But unlike the competitive markets Stigler analyzed, where information search reduces seller power, in health insurance the search itself generates extractable value for the seller. BCBS does not just process federal employees' claims; it captures the behavioral signal of which conditions they fear, which treatments they research, and which providers they consider. The tracking tools convert patient anxiety into advertiser-ready data. The insurer profits from the denial (reduced claim payouts), from the appeal (administrative fees), and from the search behavior generated by the denial (third-party data sales). Each stage of the patient's experience is monetized.

Game Theory Concept
Information Extraction as Multi-Stage Rent Capture

George Stigler's (1961) information economics demonstrated that the cost of consumer search creates seller market power. In health insurance, this dynamic is amplified by a unique structural feature: the entity adjudicating claims also operates the information infrastructure through which patients research their options. When BCBS embeds TikTok and Meta tracking pixels on the website 5.5 million federal employees use to look up physicians and conditions, the insurer extracts value at three stages: (1) the claim decision itself (deny or delay, reducing payouts), (2) the appeal process (administrative costs borne by the patient, with 82% overturn rates suggesting most denials are strategic rather than clinical), and (3) the behavioral data generated by the patient's search for information about their condition, providers, and rights. This is not simple information asymmetry; it is multi-stage rent capture, where each layer of the patient's engagement with the system generates revenue for the entity controlling access. The game-theoretic implication is that reforms targeting stage 1 (denial rates, gold cards, AI bans) leave stages 2 and 3 intact. A patient whose PA is approved on the first request still generates trackable search data. A patient who never files a claim but researches symptoms on an insurer's website still produces monetizable behavioral signals. The information asymmetry is not a market failure to be corrected; it is a revenue stream to be optimized.

The surveillance dimension also reveals why transparency reforms may produce perverse outcomes. CMS-0057-F requires insurers to publicly report denial metrics beginning in 2027, which should reduce information asymmetry between patients and plans. But if the reporting infrastructure requires patients to interact more frequently with insurer-controlled digital platforms to access their PA data, claims history, and appeal status, the reform could simultaneously increase the behavioral data available for extraction. The Allina Health settlement demonstrates the risk: a health system's patient portal, ostensibly designed to improve access and transparency, functioned for eight years as a data pipeline to third parties. The patients using the portal to check test results, schedule appointments, and pay bills were generating commercially valuable health data with every click. Transparency becomes a data-harvesting mechanism when the platform delivering transparency is controlled by the entity with the incentive to harvest.

The Governance Reckoning: When Every Watchdog Fails Simultaneously

The individual failures documented throughout this analysis converge in a shareholder derivative lawsuit filed in Minnesota federal court in August 2026. Rhode Island's public employee retirement system and Swedish asset manager Länsförsäkringar Fondförvaltning AB, which holds more than $123 million in UnitedHealth stock, sued the company's board on behalf of the corporation itself, alleging that directors and officers "missed red flags of misconduct and serious regulatory concerns, yet took no action to ensure compliance." The amended complaint reveals a pattern that transforms isolated incidents into a systemic governance thesis: when UnitedHealth acquired health data and payments company Change Healthcare for $7.8 billion, the subsequent cyberattack exposed the private data of nearly 200 million Americans in the largest health data breach in U.S. history. But the more damaging allegation concerns what the company allegedly did with its own internal controls. According to the complaint, an internal audit program discovered $200 million in Medicare payments "that weren't supported by patients' diagnoses." Rather than remediate the finding, the complaint alleges that CEO and board chair Stephen Hemsley "supported the decision to eliminate an audit program entirely, ensuring that the fraud could continue undetected."

The governance failures extend across every domain of the company's operations. In May 2026, Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell sued UnitedHealthcare for defrauding MassHealth, the state's Medicaid program, out of more than $100 million. The complaint alleges that between 2015 and 2025, UnitedHealthcare systematically "upcoded" patients in its Senior Care Options plan, making older adults appear sicker than they were to secure higher payments. Former nurses reported being encouraged to inflate diagnoses: occasional headaches classified as migraines, patients assigned anxiety or depression diagnoses without corresponding treatment plans, members categorized at the highest-cost Level 3 who did not meet the criteria. Campbell called it a "growth-at-all-costs strategy." The top UnitedHealthcare executive in Massachusetts allegedly quit under the pressure. The lawsuit seeks treble damages under the Massachusetts False Claims Act, potentially tripling the recovery to $300 million. In a separate proceeding, a federal judge in Minnesota granted final approval in June 2025 to a $69 million settlement in Snyder v. UnitedHealth Group, the largest-ever ERISA settlement for breach of fiduciary duty arising from failure to remove underperforming investment options. The 350,000 participants in UnitedHealth's $15 billion 401(k) plan had been locked into a Wells Fargo target date fund suite that underperformed comparable options by 70 to 90 percent over a decade. A 2018 email from Chief Financial Officer John Rex revealed that he personally overruled the recommendations of the plan's own investment committee to keep the Wells Fargo funds, reasoning that the business relationship between UnitedHealth and Wells Fargo, a major customer and financier, justified the retention. The governance mechanism designed to protect employees' retirement savings was subordinated to the company's commercial interests by the officer responsible for financial oversight.

The cumulative picture includes UnitedHealth's $8.3 billion loss from its complete exit from Latin America, a strategic retreat from the Banmedica acquisition ($2.8 billion in 2018, sold for $1 billion in 2025/2026) and prior divestments in Brazil and Peru. Three simultaneous DOJ investigations (criminal Medicare fraud, civil Medicare fraud, and the Zelis/MultiPlan antitrust probe) continue without producing penalties. The $69 million ERISA settlement, the $100 million MassHealth fraud allegation, the $200 million internal audit suppression, the $8.3 billion international write-off, the Change Healthcare breach affecting 200 million Americans, and the ongoing DOJ triple-probe are not separate governance failures. They are expressions of the same structural condition: an organization where the mechanisms meant to catch self-dealing are controlled by the actors they are meant to constrain.

Game Theory Concept
Agency Cost Cascade: Multi-Domain Governance Failure

Jensen and Meckling's (1976) theory of the firm describes agency costs as the gap between the interests of managers and shareholders. In a standard principal-agent model, internal controls (audits, investment committees, compliance programs) exist to narrow this gap. UnitedHealth's multi-front governance failures reveal what happens when the monitoring mechanisms themselves are captured. When the CFO overrules the investment committee to benefit a business partner (ERISA), when the CEO supports eliminating an audit that found $200 million in unsupported billing (Medicare), when internal clinical assessments are manipulated to inflate payments (Medicaid), and when a $7.8 billion acquisition proceeds without adequate cybersecurity integration (Change Healthcare), the common thread is not incompetence but incentive alignment: each "failure" served the interests of the managers who controlled the governance mechanisms. The game-theoretic implication is that once a single monitoring channel is captured, the marginal cost of capturing the next approaches zero, because the institutional infrastructure for self-correction has already been compromised. The shareholder derivative suit is, structurally, an attempt by the actual principals (pension funds, asset managers) to re-establish monitoring from outside the captured governance apparatus. Whether courts can substitute for failed internal controls is an open question, but the filing itself demonstrates that the agency cost cascade has reached the point where residual claimants no longer trust any internal mechanism to represent their interests.

The Pharmacy Firewall: Point-of-Sale Denial as Silent Attrition

While the political debate over prior authorization focuses on procedure-level denials between providers and insurers, a parallel denial infrastructure operates at the pharmacy counter, where the patient stands alone. A JAMA study published July 9, 2026, by Levy and colleagues at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health and the American Enterprise Institute analyzed more than 2 million first-fill prescription attempts for brand-name drugs without generic alternatives across commercial insurance, Medicare, Medicaid, and ACA marketplace plans. Their findings reveal a denial trend that has accelerated even as procedure-level PA reforms dominate headlines: initial rejection rates for brand-name prescriptions rose from 24.3% in 2018 to 40.7% in 2024, a 67% increase over six years.

The consequences of this pharmaceutical friction are measurable and severe. Of patients whose prescriptions were initially rejected, 48.4% never filled the prescribed medication or any drug in the same therapeutic class within 90 days. They simply gave up. Among those who persisted and eventually obtained treatment, the average delay was 12.2 days after initial rejection. A companion study in JAMA Health Forum by Wang and colleagues found that of branded drug prescriptions initially rejected by prior authorization, only 11% were resolved on the same day. Forty-four percent were eventually approved after a median wait of six days, and 45% were ultimately denied outright. Female patients and those with multiple chronic conditions had lower approval rates, creating a vulnerability gradient that tracks the disparities documented in the JACC and JAMA Network Open studies discussed earlier.

The rejection rates varied sharply by coverage type and drug class. Exchange plans and Medicaid managed care plans posted the highest rejection rates at 48.7% and 49.8% respectively, meaning roughly half of all first-fill attempts for brand-name drugs in these populations were blocked at the pharmacy counter. Medicare plans had the lowest rates, though still substantial. The most striking class-specific finding involved GLP-1 receptor agonist drugs (Wegovy, Ozempic, and similar weight-loss and diabetes medications): insurers rejected 85% of initial fill attempts for these drugs, the highest-profile and highest-cost therapeutic class in American medicine. Of all initial rejections across drug classes, 14.8% were due to formulary exclusions (the drug simply not covered) and 17.2% were triggered by prior authorization or step therapy requirements.

The strategic logic of the pharmacy firewall is distinct from procedure-level PA. When an insurer denies a surgical procedure or imaging study, the provider serves as an institutional advocate who can file appeals, submit peer-to-peer reviews, and escalate through established channels. At the pharmacy counter, the patient is the sole actor. The pharmacist cannot appeal on the patient's behalf. The prescribing physician is not present. The patient faces a binary choice: leave with the medication or leave without it. This structural isolation is the mechanism's design advantage. It shifts the denial interaction from an institutional context (where providers have organized collective opposition, as the Provider Revolt subsection documents) to an atomized individual context where the 48.4% abandonment rate becomes the system's most reliable output.

Game Theory Concept
Point-of-Sale Friction as Mechanism Design: Switching Costs and Strategic Attrition

Klemperer's (1987, 1995) theory of switching costs demonstrates that firms can extract supracompetitive rents by raising the cost of customer exit, even in nominally competitive markets. The pharmacy firewall is a variant: the insurer does not raise the cost of switching insurers but the cost of completing a single transaction within the existing relationship. Each rejected prescription imposes search costs (calling the insurer, waiting for the prescriber to submit PA), delay costs (12.2 days average), and psychological costs (navigating the system alone at the pharmacy counter). The 48.4% abandonment rate represents the strategic payoff: nearly half of denied patients absorb the cost of foregone treatment rather than incur the cost of navigating the appeal. The game-theoretic insight is that this mechanism is structurally resistant to the reforms targeting procedure-level PA. Gold card programs, electronic PA mandates, CMS-0057-F transparency rules, and state AI denial bans all address the provider-insurer interaction. The pharmacy counter operates in a different strategic space, where the patient, not the provider, bears the full friction. As procedure-level reforms reduce visible denials, the rational insurer response is to shift utilization management pressure to the point of sale, where the patient has no institutional advocate and the abandonment rate serves as a silent, self-enforcing denial mechanism that generates no appeals, no lawsuits, and no headlines.

The First Report Card: Cross-Market Data Confirms the Pattern

On August 13, 2026, KFF published the first comprehensive analysis of standardized prior authorization data collected under CMS-0057-F, which required insurers to publicly report denial metrics starting March 31, 2026. For the first time, the same methodology applied across three markets: Medicare Advantage, Medicaid managed care, and the ACA Marketplace. The results confirmed what the preceding analysis predicts: denial is not an aberration in one market segment but a structural feature of the business model, with rates varying by insurer identity rather than clinical necessity.

In 2025, insurers denied at least 1 in 8 standard prior authorization requests across all three markets. Medicare Advantage denial rates averaged 12%, ranging from 5% (Elevance) to 17% (UnitedHealth). Managed Medicaid averaged 14%, ranging from 2% (LA Care Health Plan) to 23% (Independence Health Group). The ACA Marketplace posted the highest rates at 18%, from 3% (GuideWell) to 25% (Centene). When denied patients appealed, the original decision was overturned two-thirds (67%) of the time in Medicare Advantage, nearly half (47%) in Medicaid managed care, and 43% in ACA Marketplaces.

Three patterns are structurally significant. First, UnitedHealth's 17% denial rate in Medicare Advantage is the highest among major MA insurers by a substantial margin, more than three times Elevance's 5%. This is the same UNH that has committed to a 30% PA reduction in 2026, eliminated rural provider PA, and cut two-thirds of pediatric PA. The KFF data shows what is left after those reductions. Second, the appeal overturn rate of 67% in MA means approximately two-thirds of denials are reversed when challenged, an efficiency rate that no legitimate quality-control mechanism would tolerate. A manufacturing process with a 67% false-rejection rate would be redesigned immediately. Third, the vast majority of denied patients never appeal at all: prior OIG data shows only about one-third of patients appeal in Medicare Advantage, meaning roughly 11% of all MA patients who receive PA denials are denied care they are entitled to and never recover it.

The same week the KFF report landed, the American Economic Liberties Project published a policy brief titled "Ban Prior Authorization: Ending Big Medicine's Strategy to Boost Profits by Denying Medically Necessary Care." The report, by Hannah Garden-Monheit and Emma Freer, abandoned the reform-and-regulate framework entirely. Instead, it proposed a federal ban on prior authorization for any entity with a financial conflict of interest, which would cover every vertically integrated insurer in the market. Prior authorization would be permitted only in "rare, narrow circumstances where no conflict of interest is present," with a blanket prohibition on AI-based denials. The report was personal: Garden-Monheit's father was enrolled in a UnitedHealth Medicare Advantage plan when he was denied intensive rehabilitation following a leg amputation after cancer treatment. He died.

The AELP report's most striking finding was the polling data: 71% of U.S. voters, including 76% of Democrats and 69% of Republicans, would support legislation prohibiting private insurers from using prior authorization at all. The bipartisan margin exceeds the consensus for most health care proposals in a polarized electorate. The report quantified the administrative destruction: prior authorization now consumes the equivalent of 99,290 full-time physicians and advanced practice clinicians, more than the nation's entire physician shortage, at a cost of $32.7 billion annually. By this accounting, the system designed to save money costs more to administer than many of the treatments it denies.

Game Theory Concept
Goodhart's Paradox: When the Scorecard Becomes the Strategy (Goodhart 1975 / Campbell 1979)

Goodhart's Law holds that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Campbell's Law extends this: the more a quantitative social indicator is used for decision-making, the more it will be subject to corruption pressures. CMS-0057-F created the first standardized cross-market PA denial scorecard. The KFF August 2026 report is the first analysis. The question is whether disclosure produces accountability or gaming. UNH's behavior suggests the answer is already apparent: its ACA denial rate dropped 15 points to converge precisely to the national average (the Convergence Trap, discussed earlier), while its MA denial rate of 17% remains the highest among major insurers. The rational insurer response to mandatory disclosure is not to reduce denials to the clinically defensible floor, but to reduce them to the politically defensible mean. The scorecard becomes a coordination device: insurers that are above the average face pressure to converge downward, but insurers that are below it face no pressure to remain there. The equilibrium prediction is a clustering around the mean, not a race to zero, because the scorecard rewards strategic compliance, not clinical excellence. And the 67% appeal overturn rate confirms the scorecard is measuring the wrong thing: it reports denial rates, not wrongful denial rates. A system that denies 12% of requests and reverses 67% of appeals is wrongfully denying approximately 8% of all requests, but the scorecard treats the initial denial and the appeal reversal as separate metrics rather than a single quality indicator.

Section IV

The Moral Calculus: Statistical Lives Saved

How many people are alive today because of what happened on December 4, 2024?

The question sounds provocative, but the methodology is standard. The U.S. federal government routinely prices the reduction of mortality risk when evaluating policy. The Department of Transportation's current Value of a Statistical Life is $14.2 million per person in 2025 dollars. Every highway speed limit, every air quality regulation, every drug approval timeline contains this calculation implicitly. The question here is whether the post-shooting reforms reduced mortality risk enough to be quantified.

Even under conservative assumptions, the answer is yes.

The Verified Inputs

Six bodies of peer-reviewed evidence anchor this estimate:

1. Reform volume. 6.5 million prior authorization requirements eliminated industry-wide between June 2025 and April 2026 (AHIP/BCBSA pledge tracking, verified by AJMC). UnitedHealthcare committed to an additional 30% reduction in 2026 and separately eliminated PA for approximately 1,500 rural hospitals and two-thirds of pediatric PA requirements for members under 18. These UNH-specific cuts are additive to the 6.5 million industry total, but their exact volume has not been disclosed publicly.

2. Denial mortality. At Memorial Sloan Kettering in 2023, researchers analyzed 1,752 prior authorization requests for cancer pain medication. Of the 62 patients whose requests were denied, 3 died within two weeks (4.8%). Another 14 ended up in the emergency department (22.6%) and 4 transitioned to hospice (6.5%). The study's lead author, Dr. Fumiko Chino, called these numbers, derived from one of the best-resourced cancer centers in the world, a "gross underestimate of the human impact." Notably, 89.4% of all requests were ultimately approved, suggesting most denials function as delay mechanisms rather than clinical gatekeeping. The OIG's June 2026 data corroborated this pattern at scale: across 19 Medicare Advantage organizations, 95% of appealed skilled nursing facility denials were overturned. naviHealth's overturn rate was 97%.

3. Treatment delay mortality. A Cleveland Clinic analysis of 3,672,561 cancer patients found that each week of delayed treatment initiation increased absolute mortality risk by 1.2% to 3.2%, depending on cancer type and stage. For stage I non-small cell lung cancer, five-year survival dropped from 56% to 43% when treatment was delayed more than six weeks. A separate BMJ systematic review of 1,272,681 patients across 34 studies found a 6-8% mortality increase per four-week surgical delay for breast and colorectal cancers.

4. Physician-reported harm. The AMA's 2024 survey of 1,000 practicing physicians found: 24% reported that prior authorization had led to a serious adverse event (hospitalization, permanent injury, or death). 78% said patients abandon treatment due to PA barriers. Practices spend an average of 12 hours per week processing 43 prior authorizations, with 65% of physicians waiting at least one business day for a decision. An updated AMA survey cited by the Medical Society of the State of New York in May 2026 found the numbers worsening: 26% of physicians now report PA has resulted in a serious adverse event, 95% say it delays access to necessary care (up from 94%), and 79% report patients abandon treatment because of authorization challenges (up from 78%).

5. Patient-reported harm. The Commonwealth Fund's 2025 Affordability Survey of 4,589 privately insured adults found: 28% of those denied prior authorization reported their health problem worsened. 41% experienced a care delay. 21% of all respondents reported at least one denial in the past year.

6. Denial rates. Prior authorization denial rates range from approximately 4.2% (Anthem) to 13% (CVS/Aetna), with an estimated industry average near 10%.

The Calculation

Start with the reform volume: 6.5 million eliminated PA requirements. At an average 10% denial rate, approximately 650,000 denials did not occur. The question is what fraction of those avoided denials would have resulted in death.

Three estimates, ranging from conservative to aggressive. All are Fermi estimates, transparent in their assumptions and wide in their uncertainty. This is health economics, not epidemiology.

Conservative Estimate
500 to 1,500 Statistical Lives Saved Per Year

Apply only the delay-elimination effect. Even approved PAs cause delay: 65% of physicians report waiting at least one business day, 26% report three or more days. For the estimated 15-20% of eliminated PAs involving cancer or cardiovascular treatment (roughly 975,000 to 1.3 million procedures), eliminating even a one-week delay yields measurable mortality reduction. Using the lower bound of the Cleveland Clinic coefficient (1.2% per week): 975,000 × 1.2% = 11,700 patients whose mortality risk decreased by approximately one percentage point. Scaling for the fraction where delay was genuinely life-threatening: 500 to 1,500 lives.

Central Estimate
2,000 to 5,000 Statistical Lives Saved Per Year

Include denial mortality. 650,000 avoided denials. The MSK data shows 4.8% two-week mortality among denied cancer patients, but most denials involve less immediately lethal conditions. Adjusting to a cross-condition average of 0.3% to 0.8% yields 1,950 to 5,200 lives from denial elimination alone. Add the treatment abandonment pathway: the AMA reports that 78% of physicians say patients abandon treatment due to PA. If 10% of the 6.5 million affected patients would have abandoned treatment, and 2% of those abandonment events involved conditions with meaningful mortality risk, that adds another 1,300 lives (with substantial overlap with the denial count). Net central range: 2,000 to 5,000 lives.

Upper Bound
8,000 to 12,000 Statistical Lives Saved Per Year

Include secondary cascade effects. The Commonwealth Fund's 28% "worsened health" figure encompasses chronic disease progression that manifests as excess mortality over a one-to-five year horizon. Apply a 0.5% mortality multiplier to the 182,000 patients (28% of 650,000 denied) whose conditions worsened: 910 additional deaths over the follow-up period. Add the behavioral chilling effect: patients who avoided seeking care entirely due to prior PA experience (not captured in reform data). Incorporate the full AMA signal (7% of physicians reporting at least one patient death from PA). Upper bound: 8,000 to 12,000 statistical lives per year. This estimate carries the widest uncertainty and should be treated as a ceiling, not a point estimate.

What This Means for the Game Theory

Federal policy already embeds the Value of a Statistical Life into its decisions. Every regulation that trades economic cost for reduced mortality contains this math. The post-Thompson reform is unusual only in that the triggering event was an act of violence rather than a regulatory impact analysis.

In game-theoretic terms, the pre-shooting equilibrium imposed a cost in statistical lives (from unnecessary denials, delays, and treatment abandonment) that exceeded the cost of the new equilibrium (reduced insurer profitability and higher security spending). Even under conservative assumptions, the old equilibrium was destroying value at a rate of billions of dollars per year in VSL terms. The industry chose that equilibrium because the destroyed value was dispersed across millions of patients rather than concentrated in a line item on a quarterly earnings report.

The shooting concentrated the cost.

Limitations of This Estimate

This is a Fermi estimate, not an epidemiological study. First, the 6.5 million figure is self-reported by insurers through AHIP and unaudited. Whether it represents genuine elimination of PA requirements or reclassification of existing processes is unknowable with current data. Second, the MSK mortality data comes from cancer pain medication denials at a single institution. Extrapolating to all denied conditions is aggressive; the cross-condition adjustment (0.3-0.8% in the central estimate) reflects the authors' best judgment, not a measured figure. Third, the Cleveland Clinic delay-mortality coefficients cover early-stage solid-tumor cancers specifically. Applying them to all PA-affected treatments overstates mortality impact for lower-acuity conditions.

Fourth, and most important: the causal chain is indirect. The reforms were triggered by the shooting, but "triggered by" is not "caused by" in a rigorous counterfactual sense. Some reforms were already in regulatory pipelines (the CMS Interoperability Rule was finalized before December 2024; the Improving Seniors' Timely Access to Care Act evolved from a bill first introduced in 2022). The shooting accelerated the timeline. How many reforms would have happened anyway, and on what schedule, is unknowable. Fifth, this estimate does not account for potential negative effects of PA reform, such as increased utilization of low-value care, which could have mortality implications through higher premiums or reduced coverage over time. Sixth, the lives-saved estimate applies only to the population that retains coverage. The simultaneous contraction of the insured population through Medicaid work requirements (CBO: 5.2 million by 2034), UNH's strategic MA membership retreat (1.1 million in 2026 alone), and the ACA Marketplace enrollment collapse (3 million effectuated drop, KFF projecting 8.2 million additional uninsured) means the denominator is shrinking from three directions. The PA reforms benefit those who remain covered; those who lose coverage face a different and potentially worse mortality calculus. The dual denominator erosion, Medicaid cuts from above and ACA premium increases from below, means the population for whom PA reform matters most may be contracting faster than the reform expands access within it. Seventh, the analysis focuses on medical and surgical prior authorization. The GAO's finding that CMS has never audited behavioral health PA denials, combined with the suspension of MHPAEA enforcement, means the reform ratchet may be displacing denial pressure onto behavioral health patients, a population with two-to-three-times higher denial rates, lower appeal capacity, and conditions where treatment delays measured in hours, not days, produce irreversible harm. The lives-saved calculation does not account for this substitution effect.

We present these numbers not as conclusions but as a framework for quantifying the costs the old equilibrium imposed and the benefits the new one may deliver. The uncertainty is genuine. The direction is not.

Section V

Implications

The Thompson shooting did not just change the health insurance industry. It demonstrated something about the structural limits of systems that create populations with nothing to lose.

The Broader Pattern

Any industry whose business model depends on denying resources to people facing existential outcomes faces a version of this game-theoretic problem. The specific conditions are:

1. Asymmetric stakes. One party risks profit margins. The other risks their life, their child's health, or their family's financial survival.

2. Failed release valves. The official mechanisms for redress (appeals, regulators, courts) are designed to be difficult enough that most people give up. The 0.2% appeal rate is not a bug; it is the system working as designed.

3. Information opacity. The decision-maker (insurer, algorithm) operates behind proprietary walls. The affected party cannot see or understand why they were denied.

4. Concentration of authority. Decisions affecting millions of lives flow through identifiable executives and institutions. This creates focal points for grievance.

The Uncomfortable Conclusion

The Thompson shooting produced more concrete reform in six months than decades of legislative effort. The prior authorization reform pledge, the mandatory disclosure rules, the 6.5 million fewer denials, the OIG investigations confirming systematic wrongful denial, the CEO resignation, the $190 billion in market value destroyed. None of this happened because of lobbying, lawsuits, or elections. It happened because one act of violence made the cost of the status quo viscerally real to the people who profited from it.

This is not an endorsement. It is an observation about incentive structures. When a system creates a population with diminishing reasons to cooperate, and the mechanisms for peaceful redress are deliberately inadequate, the system has engineered its own instability. The question was never whether someone would eventually act outside the rules. The question was what would change after they did.

The insurance industry's fundamental miscalculation was treating all players as if they were operating in a repeated game with a future worth protecting. For the subset of people whose future the industry itself has foreclosed, that assumption was always false. December 4, 2024 was the day the industry learned it empirically.

Where This Goes

The most likely outcome is Path A plus Path C: voluntary reform accelerated by escalating regulation, driven by the political reality that inaction is no longer viable when a CMS Administrator publicly cites violence as the catalyst for change. The 2024 Interoperability and Prior Authorization Rule's public disclosure requirements went live on March 31, 2026, forcing insurers to publish denial rates, decision times, and appeal outcomes for the first time. Early analysis by KFF found the data frustratingly aggregated, but the precedent is set: opacity is being dismantled. In April 2026, CMS extended the framework to prescription drugs with CMS-0062-P, requiring electronic PA and shorter decision timeframes for drug coverage, a regulatory expansion that no industry lobby has publicly opposed. Five distinct bills are moving through Congress targeting Medicare and marketplace plans. The Improving Seniors' Timely Access to Care Act mandates electronic PA workflows (298 House cosponsors, 42-0 and 45-0 committee votes, advancing to House floor). The Prior Authorization Accountability Act forces public denial-rate disclosure. The Patient Refunds for Bad Denials Act creates financial penalties for high denial rates. The Medicare Advantage Improvement Act establishes compliance scoring with automatic payment cuts. The Stop Deadly Denials Act would ban prior authorization in Medicare Advantage entirely. A sixth legislative front has opened on employer-sponsored plans: the DeSaulnier four-bill ERISA package targets the 130 million Americans whose coverage has been largely invisible to federal enforcement, creating DOL consumer assistance, mandatory denial-rate disclosure (including AI use), civil penalties for appeal violations, and law school clinics for denied patients. Meanwhile, eight states have enacted binding restrictions on AI in coverage decisions, with over 25 more considering similar measures, and California's SB 363 threatens escalating fines up to $1 million per case for insurers whose denials are overturned more than half the time. Whether Trump's AI preemption order can override state insurance regulation is the next legal battlefield.

UNH's Q2 2026 earnings offer a preview of what Path A looks like financially. The medical cost ratio improved to 86.7% (from 89.4% a year earlier), and operating earnings jumped 55% year-over-year to $8 billion, suggesting the company is finding operational efficiencies to offset the cost of denying fewer claims. Revenue of $112 billion was essentially flat, but adjusted EPS of $6.38 beat estimates by 29%. The company raised its full-year guidance to $19.50-$20.00 adjusted EPS and announced $4 billion in share buybacks. The stock has recovered to roughly $400 from its mid-2025 lows near $260. The message to Wall Street is clear: the company can be profitable under the new rules, even if the era of 33% denial rates is over.

The insurance industry will survive this. It will be less profitable. Prior authorization will not disappear, but it will be faster, more transparent, and subject to meaningful oversight. Starting in 2026, CMS requires most payers to decide standard PA requests within seven calendar days and urgent requests within 72 hours, down from a previous standard of 14 days. Executive security will remain elevated permanently. And somewhere in the actuarial models, there will be a new variable that didn't exist before December 2024: the cost of creating people with nothing left to lose.

The Regulatory Ratchet

The most telling proof that the equilibrium has permanently shifted is what the Trump administration is doing. On April 14, 2026, CMS published CMS-0062-P, a proposed rule extending electronic prior authorization and transparency requirements to prescription drugs. This is a deregulatory administration expanding healthcare oversight. The rule requires impacted payers to support electronic PA for drugs, respond to requests within shorter timeframes, and report API usage metrics to CMS. Mehmet Oz, the CMS Administrator appointed by a president whose first term was defined by deregulation, called it moving PA "into the digital age, replacing fax machines and fragmented systems with real-time electronic workflows."

In game theory, a ratchet is a mechanism that allows movement in one direction but prevents reversal. The Thompson shooting created a political ratchet on insurance regulation. Before December 2024, PA reform was a niche issue championed by physician lobbies and patient advocates, easily deprioritized against industry lobbying. After December 2024, opposing PA reform became politically toxic. RFK Jr., not typically associated with progressive healthcare policy, boasted that his administration "got 80% of the insurance industry to agree to eliminate prior authorization for common medical services." The political incentive has inverted: the bipartisan premium on being seen to rein in insurance companies now exceeds the lobbying premium on protecting insurer margins.

The AMA recognized this and is pressing the advantage. On June 30, 2026, the AMA sent a 14-page letter to Oz arguing that the first round of CMS-0057-F reporting was insufficient. They want service-level and plan-level breakdowns, not just aggregated numbers. They want numeric denial counts alongside percentages. They want all functionally equivalent utilization management processes included in reporting, closing the loophole where insurers rebrand prior authorization to avoid transparency requirements. At the AMA's annual meeting, delegates voted to build an independent prior authorization database collecting denial rates, care delays, and patient harm metrics across major insurers, essentially creating a parallel accountability system that does not depend on CMS enforcement. Each of these measures ratchets the baseline forward. None of them can be rolled back without a political cost that exceeds the regulatory cost of compliance.

The ERISA Blind Spot

Most of the reform momentum has targeted the parts of health insurance that government already regulates closely: Medicare Advantage plans under CMS, ACA marketplace plans under HealthCare.gov, and state-regulated individual and small-group markets. But the largest pool of insured Americans sits in a regulatory gap. Over 130 million people get coverage through employer-sponsored health plans governed by ERISA, the 1974 federal law that preempts most state insurance regulation. These plans answer to the Department of Labor, not CMS. And the DOL, by its own Inspector General's admission, has never enforced the existing ACA requirement that ERISA plans disclose their denial rates.

In game theory, this is regulatory arbitrage: the market segment with the weakest enforcement attracts the least scrutiny. ERISA plans are not required to publish denial data on HealthCare.gov. They are not subject to CMS star ratings. They are largely immune to state AG investigations because ERISA preemption shields them from state insurance law. When an employer-plan member is denied coverage, their appeals go through a process overseen by an agency whose staff has been cut by more than a fifth under the current administration and that has never convened the advisory council required by statute. The combination of federal preemption and federal inaction creates a zone where denial rates are unknown, enforcement is minimal, and patients have fewer options than their marketplace counterparts.

On July 16, 2026, the same day UNH reported a 55% jump in operating earnings, four House Democrats introduced a package of bills to close this gap. The four bills attack the ERISA blind spot from four angles. The Consumer Health Claim Assistance Act (DeSaulnier) creates a dedicated consumer assistance program within the DOL's Employee Benefits Security Administration, funded by an annual fee on plans rather than relying on the discretionary budget that Congress has been cutting. The Health Claim Denial Transparency Act (McBath) forces the DOL to finally implement the ACA's denial-rate disclosure requirement for employer plans, including the use of artificial intelligence in coverage determinations. The Consumer Appeal Rights Enforcement Act (Lee) gives the DOL civil monetary penalty authority for appeal-process violations, closing the enforcement gap where insurers currently face no financial consequence for violating the full and fair review requirements under ERISA. And the CLINIC Assistance Act (Courtney) funds law school programs to help patients navigate denials, building institutional capacity outside government.

The game-theoretic significance is structural. The existing five-bill CMS/Medicare pipeline targets the regulated segments. The ERISA package opens a second front on employer-sponsored plans. If both succeed, insurers lose the ability to play regulatory jurisdictions against each other. The same company that eliminates PA for its Medicare Advantage members under CMS pressure would also face transparency requirements on its employer-plan denials under DOL enforcement. The arbitrage collapses. The "Denied" report that preceded these bills documented the core mechanism failure: ERISA was designed in 1974 to protect employee benefits, but its preemption clause became a shield against state-level consumer protections that hadn't been written yet. Fifty-two years later, the shield still stands. The DeSaulnier package is the first attempt to legislate around it at the federal level since the ACA's unfunded disclosure mandates went unenforced.

The Counterfactual

Would these reforms have happened without the shooting? Some would have. The CMS Interoperability Rule was finalized in January 2024, eleven months before Thompson was killed. The Improving Seniors' Timely Access to Care Act had been introduced across three Congresses without reaching the floor. The AMA had lobbied for PA reform for years. But the counterfactual matters at the margin. Before December 2024, these reforms crept forward at the pace of legislative horse-trading. After December 2024, a CMS Administrator cited "violence in the streets" as the reason for urgency. Nearly 60 insurers signed a voluntary pledge within six months. Eight states enacted binding AI denial bans in under 18 months.

The shooting did not create the reform agenda. It collapsed the timeline. Every month of delay in eliminating unnecessary prior authorizations has a body count, measured in the treatment delays and abandonments documented by the AMA, MSK, and Cleveland Clinic data. The reforms that were "already in the pipeline" might have taken another five to ten years to reach their current state. The statistical lives saved in the interim are the difference between the counterfactual timeline and the actual one.

Limitations of This Analysis

Several caveats apply. First, the denial rate data comes from ACA marketplace plans sold on HealthCare.gov, which represent roughly 2% of UnitedHealth's total claims volume. The OIG's June 2026 reports on Medicare Advantage post-acute care suggest the pattern holds in other segments: UNH denied 66-71% of LTCH and IRF requests, far exceeding the 41-42% average. But extrapolating to employer-sponsored coverage remains an open question, precisely because ERISA-regulated plans have never been required to disclose their denial rates. The DeSaulnier bills introduced in July 2026 would close this transparency gap. UNH says its overall approval rate is 98% when accounting for duplicates, eligibility, and timely submissions.

Second, this analysis treats prior authorization as uniformly harmful. That framing has a counterpoint. A University of Chicago working paper by Brot-Goldberg et al. found that Medicare prescription drug PA requirements save $95.88 per beneficiary in drug spending and that these savings exceed the administrative costs they create. The paper's authors argue that "we can't just infer waste based on the presence of administrative costs." This is a legitimate finding. But it measures cost savings from redirecting patients to generic alternatives, not the mortality cost of denying life-saving treatments. The question is not whether PA can work; it is whether the way the largest insurers deployed it was killing people.

Third, the 90% error rate and 0.2% appeal rate are allegations from a class action complaint, not independently audited figures. They are calculated from reversal rates on review, which may overstate algorithmic error if the review process applies different standards. The OIG data provides independent government corroboration: naviHealth's 97% overturn rate on SNF appeals is in the same ballpark. UNH denies that nH Predict is used for coverage determinations at all.

Fourth, this analysis treats the Thompson shooting as an inflection point, which it clearly was for stock price and public discourse. But disentangling the shooting's effect from other factors (the DOJ investigation into UNH billing practices, Medicare Advantage star rating changes, rising medical cost ratios, the Optum Rx cyberattack fallout) is methodologically difficult. The $190 billion market cap decline reflects all of these, not the shooting alone.

Fifth, game-theoretic models are simplifications. Real insurance markets involve regulators, hospital systems, pharmaceutical manufacturers, and employers as additional players, each with their own strategy sets. The two-player models presented here illuminate the core dynamics, and the Provider Revolt section extends the analysis to a three-player game where hospital systems enter as a coordinated enforcement actor. But even that extension does not capture the full ecosystem. The legal proceedings add another dimension: the December 2026 calendar creates a sequential game where Carro's double jeopardy ruling (Dec 10) shapes Garnett's federal sentencing calculus (Dec 18), and the DA's response depends on anticipating both. If double jeopardy dismisses the state case, a single guilty plea resolving all criminal liability simplifies the narrative from ongoing uncertainty to a resolved act with known consequences, altering the calculus for any future actor weighing the cost of extreme action. If the state case survives, the allocution becomes prosecution evidence, creating a novel feedback loop between federal confession and state conviction. Either outcome reshapes the deterrence function, but in opposite directions.
